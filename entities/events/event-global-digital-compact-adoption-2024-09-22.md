# Global Digital Compact adoption at the Summit of the Future (22 September 2024)

On 22 September 2024, the first day of the [Summit of the Future](https://www.un.org/en/summit-of-the-future), world leaders at the UN General Assembly in New York adopted the Pact for the Future (A/RES/79/1), a resolution whose annexes included the **Global Digital Compact** (GDC) and the Declaration on Future Generations. The GDC is the first comprehensive multilateral framework for digital cooperation and AI governance to emerge from the UN system, adopted by consensus after Russia's attempt to defer adoption was rejected; seven countries declined to support the Pact after failing to secure a last-minute amendment. A civil society coalition of [more than 55 organizations](https://www.accessnow.org/press-release/civil-society-joint-statement-on-un-global-digital-compact/) — including [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md), the [Association for Progressive Communications (APC)](../organizations/org-apc.md), and [ARTICLE 19](../organizations/org-article-19.md) — had engaged in the two-year negotiation process that produced the text.

## Context

The GDC's negotiation process was launched through Secretary-General António Guterres's 2021 "Our Common Agenda" report, which called for a new global framework for digital cooperation two decades after the [World Summit on the Information Society](https://www.itu.int/net/wsis/) (WSIS). Sweden and Zambia served as co-facilitators for the intergovernmental negotiation (Zambia replacing Rwanda mid-process). The Summit of the Future — held on 22–23 September 2024 at UN Headquarters, with [action days on 20–21 September](https://www.un.org/en/summit-of-the-future) — was the culminating multilateral event at which the Pact, GDC, and Declaration were all adopted together as a single package.

Civil society organised early and substantively. By July 2024, [more than 55 organizations and individuals](https://www.accessnow.org/press-release/civil-society-joint-statement-on-un-global-digital-compact/) — led by [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md), [ARTICLE 19](../organizations/org-article-19.md), and the [Association for Progressive Communications](../organizations/org-apc.md) — had co-signed a joint brief demanding: stronger mainstreaming of international human rights law throughout the document; surveillance systems governed by the principles of legality, legitimacy, necessity, and proportionality; explicit support for end-to-end encryption, pseudonymity, and anonymity; meaningful multistakeholder participation in all GDC consultations and implementation; and opposition to centralizing internet governance functions in UN New York offices at the expense of civil society and Global South state participation.

## What the GDC contains

The adopted GDC is non-binding and spans eight thematic areas: bridging the digital divide through universal connectivity; preventing internet fragmentation to preserve an open, free, and secure internet; advancing data protection and data governance frameworks; affirming human rights online; enhancing international AI governance; promoting digital public goods and the digital commons; advancing sustainable development through digital technologies; and addressing cybersecurity.

The **AI governance section** is the GDC's most structurally significant contribution for the make-AI-good movement. It establishes three new multilateral instruments:

- The **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** (IPAI) — a body providing independent scientific evidence for digital policy, modelled on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), designed to remain independent from governments and the UN system
- The **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** — a forum for states and stakeholders to develop convergent approaches to AI governance across jurisdictions
- The **AI Governance for Humanity Lab** — a capacity-building and research institution

On **human rights online**, the GDC affirms the explicit application of the international human rights framework to digital technologies — a provision the post-adoption civil society joint statement (December 2024) identified as the text's clearest [multilateral achievement](https://www.accessnow.org/press-release/joint-statement-civil-society-global-digital-compact-implementation/). The GDC also affirms the multistakeholder model and the [UN Internet Governance Forum](https://www.intgovforum.org/) as central to global digital policymaking.

## Civil society assessment

The December 2024 joint statement signed by 26 organizations and 2 individual experts — including [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md), [ARTICLE 19](../organizations/org-article-19.md), the [Association for Progressive Communications](../organizations/org-apc.md), [Derechos Digitales](../organizations/org-derechos-digitales.md), and the Center for Democracy and Technology — gave a split verdict on the adopted text.

**Welcomed**: the explicit affirmation of the international human rights framework applied to digital technologies, and recognition of the multistakeholder model and the IGF as central to policymaking. These provisions place commitments that civil society had been pushing for into the multilateral record for the first time in a single binding UN resolution.

**Shortfalls**: the GDC's human rights obligations are [not "sufficiently robust nor consistently mainstreamed"](https://www.accessnow.org/press-release/joint-statement-civil-society-global-digital-compact-implementation/); the document lacks any commitment to "refrain from or cease the use of technologies that are fundamentally incompatible with international human rights"; the internet shutdown language ("refrain" from shutdowns) is weaker than existing UN General Assembly resolutions that "condemn" them; surveillance guidance is "vague" and references only "compliance with international law" without naming the principles of necessity and proportionality; the GDC "fails to adequately incorporate an intersectional gender lens"; and the climate provisions take a "very techno-solutionist approach" that ignores the environmental costs of AI infrastructure. The signatories also stated that their support for implementation "should not be interpreted as an endorsement of a process, which did not meaningfully engage with, nor incorporate, diverse civil society perspectives."

## Significance

The GDC adoption is the first moment at which a UN General Assembly resolution explicitly affirms the application of the international human rights framework to digital technologies — placing the legal anchor that civil society organizations including Access Now, APC, and Article 19 had been pressing for across the two-year negotiation. The Independent International Scientific Panel on AI is the GDC's most structurally significant AI governance output: an IPCC-style scientific body for AI, the first such instrument at UN system level.

The adoption is the endpoint of the civil society advocacy track that organizations in this corpus — notably APC (through the IGF, ECOSOC, and WSIS+20 processes), Access Now (through the KeepItOn and Ban Biometric Surveillance campaigns), and Article 19 (through the AI content moderation campaign) — had engaged over 2023–2024. The civil society coalition's split verdict — human rights framework affirmed but weakly implemented, AI governance bodies established but under-powered — sets the terms of the GDC implementation debate that will run through the remainder of the V4 period. The adjacent [Brazil AI bill civil society senate hearing (2023)](../events/event-brazil-ai-bill-civil-society-senate-hearing-2023.md) and the broader arc of civil society engagement with national and regional AI legislation provide the context in which the GDC's non-binding multilateral track sits as the global-governance layer alongside those legislative processes.
