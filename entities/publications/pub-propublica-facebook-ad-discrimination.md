# Facebook Lets Advertisers Exclude Users by Race

*Facebook Lets Advertisers Exclude Users by Race* is the [founding investigation](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-lets-advertisers-exclude-users-by-race) in [ProPublica](../organizations/org-propublica.md)'s six-year Facebook ad discrimination inquiry, published on 28 October 2016 by [Julia Angwin](../persons/person-julia-angwin.md) and Terry Parris Jr. under the [Machine Bias series](https://www.propublica.org/series/machine-bias). The investigation documented that Facebook's advertising platform allowed housing advertisers to exclude users by "Ethnic Affinity" — Facebook's internal categories for African-American, Asian-American, and Hispanic users — from the audience for housing ads, in an arrangement [a civil rights attorney characterized as "about as blatant a violation of the federal Fair Housing Act as one can find"](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-lets-advertisers-exclude-users-by-race). ProPublica demonstrated the mechanism directly: its reporters purchased a test housing ad that excluded users in those categories, and Facebook approved it. The 2016 investigation opened a six-year reporting arc with three distinct phases — a 2016–2017 inquiry establishing racial and age exclusion practices; a 2019 civil rights settlement and concurrent HUD enforcement action; and a 2022 DOJ settlement that became the first federal case successfully challenging algorithmic bias under civil rights law.

## The original investigation: racial exclusion in housing ads (2016)

The founding investigation's methodological contribution was its direct purchase of a discriminatory ad — a replicable, evidence-grade demonstration rather than a theoretical analysis of targeting capabilities. [Facebook's self-service ad platform allowed advertisers to select "Ethnic Affinities" as exclusion criteria](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-lets-advertisers-exclude-users-by-race), meaning advertisers in the housing category could filter out users Facebook had categorized as African-American, Asian-American, or Hispanic. The exclusion tool was distinct from positive targeting (showing an ad to a particular demographic) in that it allowed advertisers to remove protected groups from the ad's potential audience — the mechanism the Fair Housing Act specifically prohibits in housing advertising. ProPublica purchased the test ad and [Facebook approved it without flagging the discriminatory exclusion criteria](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-lets-advertisers-exclude-users-by-race), establishing that the capability was a normal commercial feature of the platform's housing-advertising product. Facebook's privacy manager, in response to the investigation, defended the Ethnic Affinity exclusion feature as a testing tool for multilingual campaigns but acknowledged relocating the category outside the ad system's demographics sections. On [February 8, 2017, Facebook announced an automated system to prevent advertisers from using racial categories in housing, employment, and credit ads](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-lets-advertisers-exclude-users-by-race) — a commitment ProPublica's follow-up investigations tested systematically.

## Persistent racial exclusion: the 2017 follow-up

The November 2017 follow-up ["Facebook (Still) Letting Housing Advertisers Exclude Users by Race,"](https://www.propublica.org/series/machine-bias) by Julia Angwin, Ariana Tobin, and Madeleine Varner, ran the same test a year after the initial investigation and after Facebook's promised fix. The reporters found that housing advertisers could [still exclude users by Ethnic Affinity](https://www.propublica.org/series/machine-bias) from their ad audiences, despite the company's February 2017 public commitment to block the practice. The finding established a pattern that would recur: Facebook would make public commitments to eliminate discriminatory targeting capabilities, ProPublica would test whether those commitments had been implemented, and the tests would reveal the commitments had not closed the underlying capability.

## Employment age discrimination (2017)

The December 20, 2017 investigation, [published jointly by ProPublica and The New York Times](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-ads-age-discrimination-targeting), by Julia Angwin, Ariana Tobin, and Noam Scheiber (NYT), extended the inquiry from housing to employment advertising and from race to age. Major employers including [Amazon, Verizon, UPS, Goldman Sachs, Target, and Facebook itself](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-ads-age-discrimination-targeting) used Facebook's age-targeting tools to restrict job advertisements to users in specific age ranges — Verizon targeting a financial analyst position to users aged 25–36, UPS limiting package handler advertising to users aged 18–24 — in ways employment lawyers characterized as "blatantly unlawful" exposure under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967, which prohibits bias against workers 40 and older. A structural feature of digital ad targeting identified in the piece made the practice particularly difficult for affected workers to detect: [unlike print advertising, people excluded from targeted digital job ads never discover the opportunities they have been screened out of](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-ads-age-discrimination-targeting). After being contacted by ProPublica, Amazon, Northwestern Mutual, and the New York City Department of Education changed their targeting strategies; LinkedIn modified its system to prevent age-based targeting in employment ads.

## Civil rights settlement (March 2019)

On March 19, 2019, Facebook reached a civil rights settlement with the [ACLU, the Communications Workers of America, and civil rights groups](https://www.aclu.org/news/womens-rights/facebook-settles-civil-rights-cases-making-sweeping). Under the settlement, Facebook committed to creating a separate ad portal for housing, employment, and credit (HEC) advertising — a structurally distinct interface with limited targeting options — and to prohibiting within that portal the use of age, gender, and zip code as targeting variables, as well as targeting based on Ethnic Affinity or protected-characteristic proxies. Facebook also agreed to prohibit the use of age, gender, and zip code as inputs when generating ["Lookalike Audiences"](https://www.aclu.org/news/womens-rights/facebook-settles-civil-rights-cases-making-sweeping) for HEC ad categories, and to limit geographic targeting in those categories to a minimum radius of fifteen miles. The settlement included advertiser certification requirements and a [three-year monitoring period](https://www.aclu.org/news/womens-rights/facebook-settles-civil-rights-cases-making-sweeping) under which civil rights groups could audit the platform's implementation.

## HUD's algorithmic discrimination allegation (March 2019)

One week after the civil rights settlement, on March 28, 2019, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development filed a Fair Housing Act complaint against Facebook — a concurrent enforcement action that went beyond what the settlement addressed. [HUD's complaint alleged that Facebook's ad delivery algorithms themselves discriminated against users independently of advertiser targeting choices](https://www.propublica.org/article/hud-sues-facebook-housing-discrimination-advertising-algorithms): even when advertisers did not explicitly select protected-characteristic exclusions, HUD alleged, Facebook's optimization algorithms shaped the audiences actually reached by housing ads in ways that produced discriminatory outcomes based on race, gender, ethnicity, family status, and disability. The algorithmic-discrimination allegation was a structural escalation beyond the prior investigations, which had focused on advertiser-accessible targeting tools: HUD was asserting that the platform's core optimization function was itself a Fair Housing Act violation. [HUD explicitly cited ProPublica's investigations as driver of the enforcement action](https://www.propublica.org/article/hud-sues-facebook-housing-discrimination-advertising-algorithms) — the first formal federal acknowledgment of the investigation's direct triggering role in regulatory enforcement. Facebook disputed the allegation, asserting it had "no evidence that our AI systems discriminate against people."

## Post-settlement persistence (December 2019)

The December 13, 2019 investigation by Ava Kofman and Ariana Tobin, ["Facebook Ads Can Still Discriminate Against Women and Older Workers, Despite a Civil Rights Settlement,"](https://www.propublica.org/series/machine-bias) found that age and gender discrimination in employment advertising persisted on Facebook's platform nine months after the March 2019 civil rights settlement — closing the same loop as the 2017 racial exclusion follow-up: a commitment to eliminate discriminatory capabilities had not eliminated them.

## DOJ settlement and first federal enforcement (June 2022)

On June 22, 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice reached a [settlement with Meta](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-doj-advertising-discrimination-settlement) — the first federal case successfully challenging algorithmic bias under the Fair Housing Act. The settlement required Meta to eliminate its "Special Ad Audience" tool by the end of 2022; to [modify its ad delivery algorithms to ensure that the age, gender, and estimated race or ethnicity of a housing ad's overall audience matches the eligible population's demographic composition](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-doj-advertising-discrimination-settlement); and to submit its ad systems to a third-party reviewer operating under court oversight. The [statutory penalty was $115,054 — the maximum amount permitted under the applicable law](https://www.propublica.org/article/facebook-doj-advertising-discrimination-settlement). The algorithmic-modification requirement — mandating that Meta change how the system distributes ads at the infrastructure level, not merely restricting what advertisers can explicitly select — resolved the HUD complaint's algorithmic-discrimination allegation and is the settlement's most consequential structural term: it established that the Fair Housing Act reaches the optimization function of an ad delivery system, not only its explicit targeting interface.

## Position within the corpus

The Facebook ad discrimination investigation is the corpus's primary entry closing the G7.1 accountability-journalism investigation slot under the 2026-09-01 two-on-ramp rule. It fills the on-ramp-(b) case — real original investigative journalism shedding light on AI harms — through its six-year reporting arc and its documented regulatory outcomes: a civil rights settlement, a federal Fair Housing Act enforcement action, and a DOJ settlement requiring algorithmic modification at the infrastructure level. [ProPublica](../organizations/org-propublica.md) was already in the corpus as an accountability-journalism organization admitted under the same on-ramp rule; this publication entry is the load-bearing anchor of that admission — the investigation that drove the HUD complaint cited in ProPublica's organizational entry. Within the corpus's publication layer, the investigation occupies the advertising-algorithm discrimination slot distinct from the technical-audit tradition anchored by [Gender Shades](pub-gender-shades.md): where that corpus demonstrates discriminatory system performance through technical measurement, the Machine Bias investigation demonstrated discrimination through commercial purchase of discriminatory advertising products — a distinct investigative methodology that proved more directly legible to regulatory actors as evidence of Fair Housing Act violation, and that produced the first federal algorithmic-bias enforcement action under existing civil rights statutes rather than new AI-specific law.
