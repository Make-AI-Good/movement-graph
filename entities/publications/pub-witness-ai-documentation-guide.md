# Ticks or It Didn't Happen: Confronting Key Dilemmas in Authenticity Infrastructure for Multimedia

*Ticks or It Didn't Happen: Confronting Key Dilemmas in Authenticity Infrastructure for Multimedia* is a [research report](https://lab.witness.org/ticks-or-it-didnt-happen/) published by [WITNESS](../organizations/org-witness.md) Media Lab in December 2019, examining the civil-society implications of "verified-at-capture" authentication technologies — tools that embed cryptographic signatures, geotags, and timestamps into photos and videos at the moment of recording to establish a verifiable provenance chain. Produced under the Prepare, Don't Panic initiative that WITNESS launched in response to the emerging deepfakes and synthetic media landscape, the report is the foundational civil-society analysis of content-provenance infrastructure: not a practical detection guide or a threat taxonomy, but a systematic identification of the structural tensions that any civil society actor, technologist, or policymaker must confront before deploying or mandating authenticity infrastructure at scale.

## Background and context

By late 2019, the landscape had evolved from the mid-2018 moment captured in [WITNESS's earlier convening report](pub-witness-synthetic-media-deepfakes-2018.md): commercial availability of face-reenactment and audio-synthesis tools had broadened considerably, and the initial policy response — centering on platform detection, labelling mandates, and election-integrity frameworks — was accelerating toward implementation. Simultaneously, a cluster of "verified-at-capture" tools were emerging: applications and camera software features that could cryptographically sign every image or video at the moment of recording, embedding metadata that could later prove the file had not been manipulated after capture. These tools represented a structurally different approach to the authenticity problem — prevention through provenance rather than forensic detection after the fact — and their advocates included WITNESS itself through its own ProofMode tool developed with the [Guardian Project](../organizations/org-guardian-project.md).

The [2019 report](https://lab.witness.org/ticks-or-it-didnt-happen/) takes a step back from advocacy for any specific solution and asks the harder question: what happens when authenticity infrastructure becomes widespread, and who bears the costs of its failure modes? WITNESS's framing, in the title, is deliberately double-edged — "ticks or it didn't happen" names the risk that verification checkmarks become a de facto credential, reducing unverified content to presumed fakery even when unverified content is authentic and the documenter is at risk.

## The 14 dilemmas

The report [identifies 14 dilemmas](https://lab.witness.org/ticks-or-it-didnt-happen/) organized across three structural domains.

**Participation and access.** Who can use these tools, and who can't? Inclusion/exclusion dilemmas arise from device and software requirements that create participation floors: a human rights defender in a low-resource context using a legacy Android device, or a community journalist whose device is rooted to bypass surveillance software, is structurally excluded from the authenticity credential. The surveillance dilemma is that any system creating a verifiable chain of custody between a media file and a device — and by extension a person — also creates a record that authoritarian states and corporate actors can exploit. The chilling-effect dilemma turns on whether the expectation of authenticated documentation systematically discourages witnesses in high-risk contexts from documenting at all, trading comprehensiveness for a subset of credentialed content.

**Implementation and institutional integration.** The visual-shortcut dilemma: when a verification checkmark becomes visually associated with "this is real," the absence of a checkmark becomes associated with "this may be fake" — a shortcut that systematically disadvantages civil-society documentation produced outside authenticated channels. Justice system dilemmas arise from the risk that courts or prosecutorial bodies begin requiring authenticated media as a condition of evidentiary use, shutting out documentation predating the infrastructure or captured by defenders without access. Platform integration dilemmas address whether platforms requiring authentication for distribution create a two-tier information environment that concentrates visibility among credentialed institutional actors. Data storage and ownership dilemmas concern who controls the metadata repositories on which authentication depends — private company control of the canonical verification record creates conditions for denial, revocation, or selective authentication that undermine civil society.

**Technical and operational constraints.** The complexity dilemma: tools difficult to understand correctly will be misread, and a misread verification outcome — a file failing authentication because of a compression artifact rather than manipulation — carries higher stakes than most error types. Legacy device and rooted-device dilemmas address the population of human rights defenders who cannot upgrade hardware or who have modified their devices for privacy and safety. The blockchain-trustworthiness dilemma concerns whether distributed ledger architectures used to anchor authenticity records are as reliable and neutral as their advocates claim — and whether their failure modes are visible to civil society actors before those actors have committed to the infrastructure.

## Global South centering

The [report's methodology](https://lab.witness.org/ticks-or-it-didnt-happen/) explicitly engages the asymmetry between where authenticity infrastructure is designed and where its costs are borne. The 14 dilemmas were developed through consultations with practitioners in low-resource and high-surveillance contexts as well as technologists and policy stakeholders in higher-resource environments — reflecting WITNESS's commitment, visible also in the [2018 convening](pub-witness-synthetic-media-deepfakes-2018.md), to centering communities in the Global South where the gap between the harm addressed and the harm introduced by a solution is widest. The report warns explicitly that a policy mandate or industry norm for verified-at-capture authentication — well-intentioned in a US or European election-integrity context — could impose structural harms on human rights defenders in authoritarian contexts who cannot obtain the credential without exposure.

## Position within the corpus

*Ticks or It Didn't Happen* closes the corpus's content-provenance publication gap in tandem with [pub-witness-synthetic-media-deepfakes-2018](pub-witness-synthetic-media-deepfakes-2018.md), which anchors the earlier moment: the 2018 report established the threat taxonomy and convened the field; the 2019 report interrogates the proposed solutions. Together they form the foundational WITNESS intellectual contribution to the movement's approach to synthetic media and content authenticity — the 2018 frame of "what can we do?" followed by the 2019 frame of "what are the costs and for whom?" The dilemmas the report names have proved durable: the tensions around who gets a credential, who controls the infrastructure, and what happens to unverified authentic content remain live in the C2PA governance debates that [WITNESS](../organizations/org-witness.md) has engaged since 2020, and in civil society responses to platform-level authenticity mandates in subsequent years.
