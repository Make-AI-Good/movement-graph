# AI Now 2023 Landscape: Confronting Tech Power

*AI Now 2023 Landscape: Confronting Tech Power* is a [103-page policy report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-confronting-tech-power) published by the [AI Now Institute](../organizations/org-ai-now-institute.md) on April 11, 2023, authored by Amba Kak and Sarah Myers West, with research contributions from Alejandro Calcaño, Jane Chung, Kerry McInerney, and [Meredith Whittaker](../persons/person-meredith-whittaker.md). The report's central proposition is that ["there is nothing about artificial intelligence that is inevitable"](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) — that the harms produced by deployed AI systems are the downstream outputs of a structurally concentrated industry, and that addressing those harms requires confronting that concentration rather than correcting individual systems after deployment. The report surveys eleven major policy areas, identifies what it calls seven windows for action, and classifies policy responses into three categories: bold structural reforms to pursue, widely promoted approaches to abandon as insufficient, and non-regulatory interventions — worker organizing, shareholder advocacy, and grassroots activism — to pursue in parallel with legislation.

## Big Tech's three-pillar dominance

The report's structural diagnosis turns on a proposition it states plainly: [there is no AI without Big Tech](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary). A handful of companies control the three resources that determine who can participate in AI development — and all three are structured to resist redistribution. The [data advantage](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) derives from a decade of mass behavioral surveillance: major platforms accumulated behavioral data at a scale no challenger could replicate without replicating the surveillance infrastructure that generated it, making data a moat that predates and outlasts any specific AI product. The [computing power advantage](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) is infrastructure-intensive — training large models requires expensive purpose-built hardware, specialized labor, and long supply-chain lead times, and consolidation in the chip supply chain means the same concentration dynamics operate one layer down. The [geopolitical advantage](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) is the most recent layer: as governments around the world began treating AI capabilities as strategic national assets, the handful of companies that had achieved dominance found themselves repositioned as indispensable national infrastructure — a positioning that grants regulatory forbearance in exchange for strategic access, narrowing what governments feel they can demand.

The report documents that these advantages compound: the compute-intensive character of large language models, the report argues, is not technologically inevitable but is [the specific architectural choice that most effectively translates data and compute advantages into a durable market position](https://aihub.org/2023/04/12/2023-landscape-a-report-from-the-ai-now-institute/). [Smaller businesses and startups struggle to compete](https://aihub.org/2023/04/12/2023-landscape-a-report-from-the-ai-now-institute/) not because their ideas are weaker but because the cost structure of the dominant architecture is calibrated to the balance sheet of firms that already own the data and the cloud infrastructure. The report frames the early 2023 ChatGPT moment — when the technology was being presented in public discourse as a democratizing disruption — as a case study in how this concentration works: the excitement is real, but [the economic and market-structure beneficiary of the ChatGPT cycle is the same handful of companies](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) whose existing AI investments, distribution infrastructure, and compute contracts meant they alone could move to capitalize on it.

## The seven windows for action

The report identifies seven policy areas where structural intervention is both feasible and necessary. **Unwinding data advantage** — implementing data minimization rules and scrutinizing mergers that consolidate data control — is positioned as the foundational intervention: the report argues that many AI harms are downstream of the data collection practices that trained the systems, and that [data minimization rules active in existing data protection frameworks have already produced results](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary), pointing to Italy's privacy regulator using existing EU data protection law to issue the first national ChatGPT ban as an illustration of what enforcement of existing frameworks can achieve without waiting for new legislation.

**Competition reform** — aggressive antitrust enforcement plus pending Congressional bills — the report treats as the structural complement to data regulation: mergers that consolidate data and compute require scrutiny that current merger review frameworks have not consistently applied. **Regulating large language models** involves applying documentation requirements drawn from the EU AI Act approach — not accepting the framing that LLMs are too general-purpose to regulate, but requiring disclosure of training data, capability evaluations, and deployment contexts as conditions of commercial operation.

**Displacing audits** is positioned as a correction of the previous policy cycle: the report argues that [the response of treating algorithmic audits as the primary accountability mechanism has failed](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) because audit findings rarely produce compliance and because audits are structurally incapable of catching harms that emerge from deployment context rather than from the model itself. The report advocates replacing or supplementing audits with structural bans in the highest-harm categories. **Biometric surveillance** merits [bright-line rules that future-proof the category from its changing forms](https://www.infodocket.com/2023/04/11/ai-now-institute-releases-2023-landscape-report-confronting-tech-power/): the report specifically identifies virtual reality headsets and automobiles as frontier domains where biometric data collection is expanding without a regulatory framework.

**Worker surveillance curbs** address the workplace deployment of algorithmic management — the report calls for [establishing baseline worker protections from algorithmic management and surveillance and placing the burden of proof on developers and employers](https://www.infodocket.com/2023/04/11/ai-now-institute-releases-2023-landscape-report-confronting-tech-power/). The report identifies emotion recognition as a technology that [should never be deployed in any context, particularly in the workplace](https://www.infodocket.com/2023/04/11/ai-now-institute-releases-2023-landscape-report-confronting-tech-power/), on the grounds that the technology rests on pseudoscientific premises about the relationship between facial movement and emotional state and that any deployment produces discriminatory effects. **Trade agreement protection** — preventing digital trade agreements from incorporating provisions that pre-empt national AI regulation — addresses a terrain the report identifies as largely unmonitored: trade negotiators adopting data localization prohibitions and liability immunities on behalf of industry that could be used to challenge domestic AI accountability measures.

## What to abandon: the inadequate policy responses

The report is explicit that some widely promoted approaches should be set aside. [Voluntary industry commitments and self-regulatory frameworks](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) — the report treats the decade-long cycle of AI ethics principles, codes of conduct, and industry-led multistakeholder processes as a documented case study in co-option: companies strategically adopt the language of accountability while [inserting themselves as the primary interlocutors in policy processes and ensuring any resulting standards are too general to constrain specific products or practices](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary). The Microsoft-backed Washington State facial recognition bill is cited as exemplary: [Microsoft publicly supported the bill while it was significantly weaker than the ban advocates sought](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary), allowing the company to participate in a regulatory outcome that would not constrain its product.

[Individual-consent and notice-and-choice frameworks](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) — requiring disclosure and opt-out rather than structural constraints — are treated as structurally inadequate because they place the burden of protecting against harm on individuals who lack the technical literacy, time, or realistic choice to exercise the opt-out. The report frames the widespread adoption of these frameworks as an industry achievement — they satisfy a surface demand for accountability while foreclosing more structurally constraining alternatives.

## Non-regulatory interventions: the necessary parallel track

The report's theory of change extends beyond legislative reform. [Worker organizing is positioned as a necessary parallel to policy reform](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary), not a fallback when legislation fails: workers inside AI companies, content moderators and data labellers whose work makes AI systems function, and workers subject to AI-driven algorithmic management are identified as three distinct but overlapping constituencies whose organizing creates accountability pressure that legislation alone cannot generate. [Shareholder advocacy and institutional investor pressure](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) is treated as a lever for transparency on AI risk disclosures. Grassroots activism — public campaigns, consumer advocacy, and community organizing — appears throughout the report as both a moral legitimating force and a practical pressure mechanism that has historically been necessary to convert technical policy reform from expert consensus into enacted law.

The report closes with four strategic principles it frames as orienting rather than prescriptive: [shift the burden of proof to companies to demonstrate non-harm rather than waiting for post-deployment evidence](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary); [pursue cross-policy coordination](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) to prevent companies from exploiting regulatory gaps between competition, privacy, and AI policy frameworks; [identify and counter industry subterfuge](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) by recognizing when policy language is being adopted in a form diluted enough to serve industry rather than constrain it; and [embrace a broad-based theory of change](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/2023-landscape-executive-summary) that connects legislative advocacy to organizing.

## Position within the corpus

*Confronting Tech Power* is the [AI Now Institute](../organizations/org-ai-now-institute.md)'s first annual landscape report since the organization's restructuring, establishing the format — broad multi-sector policy surveying, structural diagnosis, and an explicit distinction between what to pursue and what to abandon — that the 2025 *Artificial Power* report would carry forward. In the publications layer it sits alongside [*Discriminating Systems*](./pub-ainowinstitute-discriminating-systems-2019.md) (the 2019 AI Now Institute single-topic report on workplace diversity and AI bias) as the two load-bearing AI Now Institute entries in the corpus, but occupies a structurally different position: where *Discriminating Systems* is a focused thesis argument about one causal link (workforce composition → system discrimination), *Confronting Tech Power* is a strategic manifesto about the whole field — an attempt to provide a unified political-economic frame for AI harms that had been argued separately across labor, civil rights, antitrust, data protection, and environmental advocacy. The report's appearance on April 11, 2023 — three months after OpenAI's ChatGPT moment dominated public AI discourse — also gives the corpus a publication-side anchor for the movement's response to the generative AI turn: not treating it as a neutral technological disruption but as a power-concentration event whose beneficiaries were predictable from the existing industry structure.
