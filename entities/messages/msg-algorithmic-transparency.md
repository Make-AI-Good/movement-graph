# Algorithmic transparency

**Algorithmic transparency** is the civil society demand that algorithmic systems deployed in consequential domains — employment, housing, credit, healthcare, education, public services — be subject to mandatory public disclosure, pre-deployment impact assessments, and third-party audit requirements. The demand is systemic: it targets how entire classes of systems operate, not just an individual's right to understand a specific decision made about them. That individual right is the [right to explanation](msg-right-to-explanation.md) framing — distinct in focus, legal basis, and scope: the right to explanation gives a person legal recourse after a specific automated decision affects them; algorithmic transparency requires operators to assess and disclose their systems' design, training data, error rates, and discriminatory patterns before and during deployment, to the public and to regulators.

## The impact assessment proposal

The framing's foundational US articulation came from the [AI Now Institute](../organizations/org-ai-now-institute.md)'s [April 2018 report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/algorithmic-impact-assessments-report-2) "Algorithmic Impact Assessments: A Practical Framework for Public Agency Accountability," authored by Dillon Reisman, Jason Schultz, Kate Crawford, and Meredith Whittaker. The report modeled a mandatory pre-deployment assessment requirement on environmental impact statements: before deploying an automated decision system, a public agency would assess the system's potential harms, document its design and training data, and release findings publicly with a mechanism for community comment and response. The report's central argument against individual-level transparency was precise: allowing individuals to audit decisions made using their own data "is insufficient to uncovering larger systemic issues" — a tool manufacturer could satisfy every individual inquiry while concealing patterns of discriminatory output visible only at population scale. That asymmetry between individual remedy and systemic harm has anchored the civil society framing ever since.

## US legislative crystallization: the Algorithmic Accountability Act

The report's impact assessment framework became the direct template for the Algorithmic Accountability Act (AAA), first introduced by Senators Cory Booker and Ron Wyden and Representative Yvette Clarke in 2019, updated and reintroduced in 2022, and reintroduced again in September 2023. Each iteration expanded coverage: the 2022 update refined algorithm coverage and prioritized consumer impacts; the 2023 version [explicitly extended coverage to new generative AI systems](https://www.booker.senate.gov/news/press/booker-wyden-clarke-introduce-bicameral-bill-to-regulate-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-make-critical-decisions-like-housing-employment-and-education) used in critical decisions alongside existing automated systems. Under all three versions, companies would be required to conduct impact assessments for high-risk automated decision systems, disclose findings to the FTC, and produce transparency reports accessible to the public. The [2023 Act attracted a civil society coalition](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/support_for_the_algorithmic_accountability_act_of_2023.pdf) of 30+ endorsing organizations — Access Now, the Center for Democracy and Technology, Color of Change, Consumer Reports, [Encode Justice](../organizations/org-encode-justice.md), [EPIC](../organizations/org-epic.md), [Fight for the Future](../organizations/org-fight-for-the-future.md), the National Hispanic Media Coalition, and others — the broadest US civil society coalition yet coordinated on this demand.

## Propagation: DSA, EU AI Act, states, foundation models

The framing's international propagation ran through two EU regulatory regimes. The Digital Services Act, fully in force February 2024, requires very large online platforms and search engines to conduct annual risk assessments and commission third-party audits under Article 42, with assessments published in meaningful form; a [joint civil society statement](https://cdt.org/insights/joint-civil-society-statement-on-meaningful-transparency-of-risk-assessments-under-the-digital-services-act/) coordinated by CDT Europe specifically demanded that these be "more than merely a 'tick box' exercise" — scrutinizable by researchers, journalists, and civil society. The EU AI Act's Articles 9–17 mandate conformity assessments, technical documentation, and transparency obligations for high-risk AI systems across employment, credit, law enforcement, and other sensitive domains — systemic disclosure requirements applied upstream from any individual interaction with an automated decision. [AlgorithmWatch](../organizations/org-algorithmwatch.md) and [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md) tracked and evaluated these obligations throughout the Act's 2024–2026 rollout.

In the US, after Congress did not pass the AAA, CDT and Consumer Reports published ["Bringing Transparency and Accountability to Algorithmic Decision Systems"](https://cdt.org/insights/bringing-transparency-and-accountability-to-algorithmic-decision-systems/) in September 2025, a state-legislative blueprint identifying mandatory impact assessments, consumer right-to-know disclosures, and human-review fallbacks as the essential regulatory elements, and tracking state bills in Colorado, Maryland, and elsewhere. In March 2026, the demand extended to AI foundation models: Representatives Beyer, Lawler, and Jacobs introduced [H.R. 8094, the AI Foundation Model Transparency Act of 2026](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8094/text/ih), directing the FTC to require developers of models with at least one billion parameters to publicly disclose training data, algorithms, limitations, and evaluation methods — the Algorithmic Accountability framing applied to the general-purpose AI infrastructure underlying automated decision systems.
