# Just transition for AI

**Just transition for AI** is the labour-movement framing that the shift to AI-governed workplaces and automated production must be governed to protect workers — not merely to maximise productivity or competitive advantage. The framing adapts the "just transition" concept from climate justice — that the decarbonisation transition must provide retraining, income continuity, and community investment for workers in affected industries — to the challenge of AI-driven automation. Where the algorithmic management and ghost-work framings document the harms of unregulated AI deployment on workers, just transition for AI makes a positive demand: that the benefits of AI productivity gains be equitably distributed, that workers retain meaningful participation in decisions about automating their work, and that no worker community bear the displacement costs of automation without corresponding public investment in retraining and economic alternatives.

## Origins and labour-movement application

"Just transition" was coined by Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers Union (OCAW) organizer Tony Mazzocchi in the United States as a demand that workers in industries being phased out for environmental or public-health reasons receive structured support rather than abandonment. The climate justice movement carried the framework into international climate negotiations in the 1990s and 2000s, embedding it in UNFCCC language and the Just Transition Declaration adopted alongside the Paris Agreement. Trade union bodies extended the same framework to AI automation as algorithmic management became a documented threat to worker conditions. [UNI Global Union's "Top 10 Principles for Ethical Artificial Intelligence"](https://uniglobalunion.org/report/10-principles-for-ethical-artificial-intelligence/) (11 February 2017) was among the first major labour-movement documents to formalise just-transition as an AI governance principle. Principle 10 of the UNI document specifically demanded that AI deployment not produce uncompensated displacement — that workers receive retraining, income protection, and genuine participation in automation decisions affecting them. The document was published by a global union federation representing 20 million workers across ICT, service, finance, and commerce sectors, and it established the template that larger confederations would amplify.

## ITUC and international adoption

The [International Trade Union Confederation](../organizations/org-ituc.md) made just transition for AI a defining demand of its intergovernmental advocacy in the 2020s. ITUC's October 2025 report ["Artificial Intelligence: What are the implications for trade unions?"](https://www.ituc-csi.org/IMG/pdf/en__ai_trade_unions_2025-2.pdf) frames algorithmic management, automated dismissals, and AI-driven job fragmentation as conditions the just-transition framework must address: it calls for binding regulation guaranteeing worker participation in AI deployment decisions, income protection for displaced workers, and enforceable rights to information about automated systems governing employment. At the [Paris AI Action Summit in February 2025](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/WO2502/S00146/ai-action-summit-trade-union-rights-are-key-to-technological-transformation.htm), the ITUC demanded that AI governance frameworks at every level include just-transition commitments as legally enforceable obligations rather than voluntary corporate social dialogue. Under South Africa's G20 presidency in 2025, ITUC demands for worker inclusion and just-transition commitments were reflected in Heads of State conclusions on AI and the future of work. The framing entered binding international law indirectly through ILO Convention 193 (the Decent Work in the Platform Economy Convention, 2026), which the ITUC coordinated: the Convention entitles platform workers to transparency about automated systems governing their working conditions — a statutory just-transition floor for the segment of the workforce most immediately managed by AI.
