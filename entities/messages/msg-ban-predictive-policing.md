# Ban predictive policing

**Ban predictive policing** is the US framing demanding categorical municipal prohibition of algorithmic systems — including PredPol/Geolitica, HunchLab, ShotSpotter, Operation LASER, and Chicago's Strategic Subject List — that direct police attention toward people or locations algorithmically designated as high-risk. The demand occupies a distinct register from the parallel framings in this corpus: where [Community Control Over Police Surveillance (CCOPS)](msg-ccops.md) pursues democratic governance over surveillance technology decisions, "ban predictive policing" pursues categorical prohibition — the question is not who approves a deployment but whether deployment is permissible at all. Its theoretical grounding is the feedback loop: predictive policing systems train on arrest records reflecting decades of racially discriminatory policing, not actual crime; they then direct police back to the same over-policed communities of color, generating more arrests and more biased data. As the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](../organizations/org-electronic-frontier-foundation.md) [framed it in September 2020](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/09/technology-cant-predict-crime-it-can-only-weaponize-proximity-policing), "technology cannot predict crime, it can only weaponize a person's proximity to police action."

## Coalition origins

The framing's organizational foundation is the [August 31, 2016 "Statement of Concern About Predictive Policing"](https://www.aclu.org/documents/statement-concern-about-predictive-policing-aclu-and-16-civil-rights-privacy-racial-justice) issued by the [ACLU](../organizations/org-aclu.md) and 16 co-signatory organizations — including [Color of Change](../organizations/org-color-of-change.md), [Center for Media Justice](../organizations/org-mediajustice.md) (now MediaJustice), the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](../organizations/org-electronic-frontier-foundation.md), Brennan Center for Justice, NAACP, and Free Press. The statement named the feedback-loop mechanism and called for transparency, community input, and racial-impact audits before deployment. This was initially a reform demand — calling for oversight rather than prohibition. The "ban" framing sharpened through 2019-2020: as LAPD ended Operation LASER (2019) and its PredPol contract (April 2020), and as George Floyd uprising debates moved abolitionist framing into mainstream political discourse, advocates shifted from demands for oversight to demands for prohibition.

## Municipal bans

[On June 23, 2020](https://oaklandprivacy.org/1st-municipal-ban-on-predictive-policing-in-santa-cruz/), Santa Cruz, California became the first US city to pass a municipal ban on predictive policing software — also banning facial recognition — through a coalition led by ACLU Northern California and Oakland Privacy after six months of city engagement. Santa Cruz Police Chief Andrew Mills supported the ban, stating he "didn't want to be in a place where we are targeting people of color based on a computer system." The LAPD ended its nine-year PredPol contract in April 2020; the [Stop LAPD Spying Coalition](../local-groups/lg-stop-lapd-spying-coalition.md), which had developed the [Automating Banishment](msg-automating-banishment.md) framing of data-driven policing, attributed the outcome to community organizing pressure. On December 15, 2020, Oakland, California [adopted express prohibitions on predictive policing analytics and biometric surveillance technologies](https://secure-justice.org/blog/first-in-the-nation-bans-predictive-analytics-biometric-surveillance-technologies) — unique in requiring racial equity tracking in annual police reports. New Orleans followed two days later with bans covering predictive analytics, cell-site simulators, facial recognition, and characteristic tracking technologies. Chicago allowed its ShotSpotter acoustic surveillance contract to expire in February 2024.

## Relationship to adjacent framings

The "ban predictive policing" demand sits at the intersection of the governance frame and the abolitionist synthesis. [CCOPS](msg-ccops.md) pursues democratic procedural authority — city council approval required before deployment — without resolving the substantive permissibility question; "ban predictive policing" resolves it as a categorical no. [Carceral tech](msg-carceral-tech.md) holds the broader apparatus — predictive policing, electronic monitoring, risk assessment, prison communication technology — under a single abolitionist analysis; the "ban" demand is the specific actionable policy vehicle at the municipal level. The [Machine Bias](msg-machine-bias.md) investigation's empirical record — that COMPAS falsely flagged Black defendants as future criminals at [nearly twice the rate of white defendants](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing) — is the evidentiary foundation the "ban" framing cites to demonstrate that algorithmic adjustment cannot neutralize the structural bias embedded in policing encounter data.
