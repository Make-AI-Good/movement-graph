# Algorithmic redlining

"Algorithmic redlining" — circulated also as "digital redlining" — names the phenomenon where algorithms and automated systems in housing, mortgage lending, and consumer financial services reproduce the geographic and racial exclusion of historic redlining without explicit racial categorization. The framing grounds contemporary AI discrimination claims in the specific legal history of the Home Owners' Loan Corporation's 1930s neighborhood-grading maps, government-sanctioned exclusion of Black neighborhoods from mortgage capital, and the civil rights enforcement framework built to address it: the Fair Housing Act of 1968 and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974. Unlike the [machine bias](msg-machine-bias.md) framing — a general critique of algorithmic risk-assessment rooted in criminal justice — algorithmic redlining is sector-specific and carries immediate legal force: a predictive model whose outputs in housing or lending correlate with race is not merely an equity concern but a potential Fair Housing Act violation, actionable under existing disparate-impact liability doctrine.

## Origin

The phrase "digital redlining" entered circulation through technology critic and professor Chris Gilliard, who [used it from at least 2016](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2017/7/pedagogy-and-the-logic-of-platforms) in educational-technology contexts — technology policies and investment decisions that enforce class and race hierarchies through differential access. The application to housing and consumer finance developed separately, through fair housing litigation and investigative journalism.

The first major civil rights enforcement action deploying this vocabulary against an algorithmic system was the [National Fair Housing Alliance](../organizations/org-national-fair-housing-alliance.md)'s lawsuit against Facebook, filed March 2018. The complaint alleged that Facebook's housing-ad algorithm permitted advertisers to exclude recipients based on race, sex, national origin, and familial status — protected characteristics under the Fair Housing Act. [The parties settled in March 2019](https://nationalfairhousing.org/2019/03/18/national-fair-housing-alliance-settles-lawsuit-with-facebook-transforms-facebooks-ad-platform-impacting-millions-of-users/): Facebook paid $2.45 million and restructured its housing-ad portal to prohibit protected-class targeting, affecting 210 million U.S. users. NFHA described the settlement as establishing "a new industry standard in Big Data and Tech concerning civil rights laws."

The lending application gained its strongest empirical foundation from [The Markup](../organizations/org-the-markup.md)'s [August 2021 investigation](https://themarkup.org/denied/2021/08/25/the-secret-bias-hidden-in-mortgage-approval-algorithms) "The Secret Bias Hidden in Mortgage-Approval Algorithms." Analyzing more than 2 million conventional mortgage applications from 2019 and controlling for income, loan amount, and neighborhood, the investigation found that lenders were 80 percent more likely to deny Black applicants than comparable white applicants, 40 percent more likely to deny Latino applicants, and 70 percent more likely to deny Native American applicants.

## Core argument

The framing makes a structural claim: algorithmic models trained on historical housing and financial data encode the geographic and economic patterns that government-sanctioned redlining produced. A credit or mortgage-underwriting model that excludes explicit race but retains neighborhood, employment type, and financial product history — all variables shaped by decades of racially discriminatory practice — will reproduce redlining's outcomes without employing its overt logic. This is the proxy discrimination argument applied specifically to sectors where the underlying civil rights violation was geographic and legally cognizable.

The legal force of the framing is that it invokes existing enforcement mechanisms rather than requiring new law. The Fair Housing Act and ECOA both apply to systems whose outputs correlate with race regardless of discriminatory intent — disparate-impact liability does not require proof of intent. NFHA's Facebook settlement and the [DOJ's June 2022 consent decree against Meta](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-secures-groundbreaking-settlement-agreement-meta-platforms-formerly-known) — the department's first Fair Housing Act action specifically targeting algorithmic discrimination, requiring Meta to develop a Variance Reduction System and abandon its "Special Ad Audience" tool — established that existing civil rights law already reaches algorithmic systems in housing.

## Propagation

The framing has propagated through sector-specific channels. In housing advertising, NFHA and DOJ enforcement actions against Facebook and Meta set the civil rights compliance template for algorithmic ad targeting. In mortgage lending, [The Markup](../organizations/org-the-markup.md)'s "Denied" series provided the evidentiary base that civil rights organizations, congressional testimony, and CFPB regulatory comments drew on. In tenant screening, the [National Consumer Law Center](../organizations/org-national-consumer-law-center.md)'s [September 2023 "Digital Denials" report](https://www.nclc.org/resources/digital-denials-how-abuse-bias-and-lack-of-transparency-in-tenant-screening-harm-renters/) — drawing on 253 advocates in 35 states — documented how AI-generated tenant screening scores create a veneer of objectivity while concealing racial disparities invisible to applicants; HUD cited it in April 2024 guidance on the Fair Housing Act's application to AI-powered tenant screening. [Upturn](../organizations/org-upturn.md) has carried the framing into FTC and FHFA regulatory comment processes. [Color of Change](../organizations/org-color-of-change.md) and the [ACLU](../organizations/org-aclu.md) have anchored it in coalition civil rights advocacy addressed to Congress and federal regulators.
