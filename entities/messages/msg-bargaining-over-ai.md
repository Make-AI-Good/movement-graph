# Bargaining over AI

**Bargaining over AI** is the trade-union demand that workers hold a legally enforceable right to collectively negotiate the terms on which AI systems are deployed in their workplaces. The demand is structurally distinct from [msg-just-transition-for-ai](msg-just-transition-for-ai.md) — which addresses post-displacement support and income continuity — and from the analytical category of [msg-algorithmic-management](msg-algorithmic-management.md) — which names the practice of software-directed work: bargaining-over-AI is specifically a claim about the *mechanism*, collective bargaining, as the instrument through which workers win enforceable control over AI deployment rather than receiving its effects as management decisions delivered unilaterally.

## CWA and the US codification

The demand crystallised in the United States through the [Communications Workers of America](../organizations/org-cwa.md). On 6 December 2023, CWA's Executive Board adopted ["Union Principles for Artificial Intelligence in the Workplace"](https://cwa-union.org/news/releases/communications-workers-america-announces-union-principles-artificial-intelligence) — developed by the National Advisory Committee on AI that CWA leadership had convened to study AI across represented workplaces. Three core principles structured the doctrine: accountability (holding management responsible for AI's impacts on workers, customers, and communities), proactive bargaining (directing CWA bargaining teams to treat AI as a priority mandatory subject of negotiation and to demand employer transparency), and early and meaningful worker voice (workers at every stage of AI design and implementation). The principles directed bargaining teams to seek specific protections against invasive surveillance, automated hiring and discipline, AI-driven work intensification, and compensation cuts. The same month, [ZeniMax Workers United–CWA announced a tentative agreement with Microsoft](https://cwa-union.org/news/releases/zenimax-workers-united-cwa-collective-bargaining-yields-first-its-kind-tentative) — described by CWA as the first-of-its-kind collective AI agreement in the US tech industry — under which ZeniMax committed to advance notice of AI deployment affecting union members and to bargain those impacts on request. By 2024, the [NewsGuild-CWA](https://newsguild.org/guild-members-are-winning-strong-protections-from-employer-pushed-ai/) reported 85–90 collective agreements with explicit AI provisions, including the Politico case where union-compelled arbitration forced management to dismantle inaccurate AI reporting tools that had been deployed without union consultation.

## In journalism: the NUJ

The [National Union of Journalists](../organizations/org-nuj.md) (UK) carries the demand in the journalism sector, calling for publishers to negotiate with NUJ branches before deploying AI tools in newsrooms and before using journalists' work to train AI models. The NUJ frames the consent-and-compensation question as a collective bargaining matter for chapel representatives to negotiate — not an individual consent question — and its guidance requires publishers to obtain explicit consent and fair compensation before using journalists' output for AI training, to refrain from using AI as a pretext for redundancies, and to ensure human editorial oversight of AI-assisted content. This addresses the journalism sector's specific AI deployment surface: generative-content tools trained on journalists' prior work and deployed to augment or replace their writing, without prior union negotiation.

## International articulation through the ITUC

The [International Trade Union Confederation](../organizations/org-ituc.md) carries the demand in international AI governance advocacy. The ITUC's AI platform frames worker and union participation in shaping AI deployment as a requirement rather than an invitation, and its October 2025 report ["Artificial Intelligence: What are the implications for trade unions?"](https://www.ituc-csi.org/IMG/pdf/en__ai_trade_unions_2025-2.pdf) calls for binding regulation guaranteeing worker participation in AI deployment decisions, enforceable information rights about automated systems governing employment conditions, and collective bargaining at all levels as the sector-specific governance mechanism for AI deployment. The framing treats collective bargaining and regulatory frameworks as complementary instruments: national legislation should establish AI bargaining as mandatory, and collective agreements should fill in the sector-specific rules that legislation cannot specify.
