# Right to explanation

**Right to explanation** is the civil society framing that people subjected to automated or AI-driven decisions have a right to receive a meaningful account of how that decision was made — what data and logic were applied, and how the individual may contest or obtain review of an outcome that significantly affects them. The framing translates an abstract transparency demand into a concrete, individually exercisable right: not merely knowledge that automation exists, but explanation of the specific decision.

## Academic origin and the GDPR debate

The phrase was introduced by Bryce Goodman and Seth Flaxman in their [June 2016 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.08813) "EU Regulations on Algorithmic Decision-Making and a 'Right to Explanation'" — circulated at the ICML Workshop on Human Interpretability in Machine Learning and later published in *AI Magazine* in 2017. Goodman and Flaxman argued that the EU's forthcoming General Data Protection Regulation created just such a right: Article 22 restricts purely automated individual decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, and Recital 71 states that data subjects "should have the right to obtain an explanation of the decision reached" in such processes. The paper triggered an immediate counter-argument. In 2017, Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt, and Luciano Floridi [published a rebuttal](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2903469) arguing that GDPR provides only a "right to be informed" of the existence and general logic of automated processing — not a right to an explanation of any specific decision. The debate established the contested terrain: the GDPR had created ambiguity where civil society needed clarity, and that gap became the demand.

## Civil society adoption: the EU AI Act campaign

From 2021, the right to explanation became a core pillar of the civil society campaign for the EU Artificial Intelligence Act. On 30 November 2021, [EDRi](../organizations/org-edri.md) coordinated 115 organisations — including [AlgorithmWatch](../organizations/org-algorithmwatch.md), [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md), [epicenter.works](../organizations/org-epicenter-works.md), [Panoptykon Foundation](../organizations/org-panoptykon-foundation.md), [Bits of Freedom](../organizations/org-bits-of-freedom.md), and Fair Trials — in a [joint statement](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/eu-artificial-intelligence-act-for-fundamental-rights/) framing the right to explanation as one of five fundamental-rights pillars the Act must enshrine. The statement specified that the right must cover decisions taken "with the assistance" of AI systems — not only purely automated decisions (the GDPR's threshold) — extending coverage to human-in-the-loop cases where an AI recommendation shapes a human decision without formally automating it. This scope extension was the campaign's key legal innovation over the GDPR framework. The same coalition had earlier demanded categorical prohibitions on the most harmful AI uses (the [Red lines framing](msg-eu-ai-act-red-lines.md)); the right to explanation was the complementary demand for those AI applications that would remain legal and deployed under the Act.

## Article 86 of the EU AI Act

[Article 86 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/), adopted 13 March 2024, formally enacted the framing. Any person subject to a decision significantly affected by a high-risk AI system may request from the deployer: a clear and meaningful explanation of the AI's role in the decision-making procedure; the main parameters and factors that influenced the output; and the level of human oversight applied. Article 86's scope is broader than GDPR Article 22 — it applies to decisions where a high-risk AI system played a significant role, not only to purely automated decisions — partially vindicating the civil society position. High-risk AI domains include employment and HR management, access to education, credit and financial services, biometric identification, law enforcement, migration, and access to essential services: the deployment areas where [the machine-bias framing](msg-machine-bias.md) and [algorithmic-hiring advocacy](msg-algorithmic-hiring.md) had documented the harms the explanation right is meant to redress.

## Enforcement from August 2026

Article 86 became enforceable from [2 August 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august), when the European Commission's AI Office and national competent authorities began applying the AI Act's transparency and fundamental-rights provisions. The same enforcement milestone activated Article 50 transparency obligations: chatbots must identify as automated systems, AI-generated content must carry machine-readable provenance marks, and deepfakes must be labelled. For the civil society organisations — [AlgorithmWatch](../organizations/org-algorithmwatch.md), [Access Now](../organizations/org-access-now.md), and [EDRi](../organizations/org-edri.md) among them — the August 2026 milestone marked the movement of the right to explanation from a campaigned-for legislative demand into an enforceable individual entitlement, a decade after Goodman and Flaxman named the frame.
