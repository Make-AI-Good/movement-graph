# Heinrich-Böll-Stiftung

The Heinrich-Böll-Stiftung (Heinrich Böll Foundation) is a German political foundation (Politische Stiftung) [established in 1997 through the merger of three predecessor organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_B%C3%B6ll_Foundation), named after the German author and Nobel laureate Heinrich Böll (1917–1985), whose work was closely associated with the West German peace movement and the emerging Green current in German politics. Headquartered in Berlin and closely affiliated with Alliance 90/The Greens — Germany's Green Party — the foundation is funded primarily through Bundestag appropriations distributed to Germany's recognized political foundations in proportion to their affiliated party's electoral performance. With [income that reached approximately €80 million by 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_B%C3%B6ll_Foundation) and [approximately 185 staff members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_B%C3%B6ll_Foundation), the foundation operates across four core commitments — ecology and sustainability, democracy and human rights, non-violence and justice, and gender democracy — through its [Berlin headquarters, 34 international offices, and 100+ partner projects in approximately 60 countries](https://www.boell.de/en/about-us). The Politische Stiftung model places it outside the conventional private philanthropic sector: rather than deploying an endowment, the foundation functions as a state-funded civil society institution whose mandate is civic education, democratic capacity-building, and international civil society engagement. Leadership since 2022 has been jointly held by Imme Scholz and Jan Philipp Albrecht as co-presidents.

## Digital policy and AI governance programme

The Heinrich Böll Foundation's AI-good footprint runs through a layered set of programming, publications, and capacity-building interventions across its regional offices. The Washington DC office leads a [Digital Policy Programme](https://us.boell.org/en/digital-policy) organized around three streams — Democracy & Digital Societies, Global Digital Governance, and Technology & Economic Transformation — that together constitute the foundation's transatlantic AI governance agenda, covering how digital technologies reshape democratic institutions, how international AI governance frameworks are negotiated, and how the technology economy affects labour and communities. Concrete programmes include *Privacy is Global*, which uses creative storytelling to bring data protection advocacy to non-specialist audiences, and the [Grow the Future network](https://us.boell.org/en/digital-policy), which convenes activists, campaigners, educators, journalists, and researchers from Germany and North America to examine the emerging politics of data centre growth, Big Tech political concentration, and alternative visions for AI infrastructure deployment.

The Brussels EU office produces sustained analytical and convening work on European AI regulation, publishing on the AI Act, Digital Services Act, and Digital Markets Act as accountability and democracy instruments, monitoring AI deregulation trends on both sides of the Atlantic, and hosting events that position civil society alongside EU policymakers. In [December 2024 the Brussels office published an analysis of European AI industrial policy](https://eu.boell.org/en/2024/12/16/european-commission-ai-industrial-policy-green-left-centre-parties) arguing that the European Commission's competitiveness framing should yield to a public-interest orientation, and in [April 2026 it hosted an "Empire of AI meets European Democracy" event](https://eu.boell.org/en/tags/AI) featuring investigative journalist Karen Hao and MEP Alexandra Geese on AI power, accountability, and the structural risks to democratic governance — placing the foundation in the space between civil society advocacy and parliamentary influence over EU AI policy.

## Global South civil society inclusion in AI governance

The foundation's most direct AI-specific grantmaking intervention is the [Global Majority AI Fellowship 2026](https://us.boell.org/en/2026/05/12/global-majority-ai-fellowship), run by the Washington DC office: a fully funded fellowship supporting civil society experts from ODA-eligible Global South countries to participate in the inaugural session of the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance in Geneva in July 2026, with a follow-up convening in New York in May 2027. The fellowship targets activists, researchers, technologists, and policy practitioners working on AI governance, digital rights, data justice, and environmental justice — covering international travel, accommodation, conference registration, and accreditation, while providing fellows with visibility and peer connections within the emerging UN AI governance infrastructure. The design reflects the foundation's wider theory of change: that Global South civil society's structural exclusion from international AI governance forums is a problem that a well-networked European political foundation, with offices and partner relationships across the Global South, is positioned to directly address.

In Latin America the foundation's Brazil office [formalized its Technopolitics Programme in 2026](https://br.boell.org/en/digital-rights), treating technology as inseparable from politics and extending the foundation's AI agenda into the Brazilian civil society context — covering algorithmic racism, content moderation, AI in electoral processes, AI's environmental costs, and AI-related socioenvironmental justice. The Brazil office has published analyses connecting AI gigantism to threats against both climate mitigation and democratic sovereignty, joining the foundation's environmental and democracy commitments directly to AI's resource demands and governance gaps. Across the foundation's 34-office global network, digital rights programming in similar shapes operates from offices in Israel, across Africa, and in South and Southeast Asia — instantiating the Global Majority AI Fellowship's theory of change at a country-office level.

## Position in the corpus's funder layer

Within this corpus the Heinrich-Böll-Stiftung occupies a structurally distinct slot from the private foundations that dominate the funder layer. As a Politische Stiftung funded by the German state, it operates under a different incentive structure from an endowment-driven private foundation — closer in character to a bilateral civic-education agency with a democratic mandate, and subject to Germany's political foundations framework which both funds and constrains its programming. That structure gives it a comparative advantage the private foundations lack: a large permanent international-office network in Global South countries, a standing relationship with Green parties and green civil society globally, and a mandate to build democratic capacity at scale rather than to select individual grantees. Its AI-good footprint is consequently more visible in programming, convening, and publications than in the discrete grant-by-grant footprint typical of the corpus's US private foundations — and its Global Majority AI Fellowship stands as the corpus's clearest example of a European political foundation using its institutional position to directly expand Global South civil society's access to the emerging global AI governance architecture.
