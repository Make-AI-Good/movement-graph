# International Development Research Centre

The International Development Research Centre (IDRC) is a Canadian federal Crown corporation established by Parliament in 1970 under the _Research Centre Act_, with a mandate to fund research into the problems of developing regions and the means of applying scientific and technical knowledge to their economic and social advancement. Headquartered in Ottawa, with regional offices in Nairobi, Dakar, Montevideo, Amman, and New Delhi, IDRC operates as a public funder with an annual budget of approximately [CAD 200 million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Development_Research_Centre), drawing entirely on parliamentary appropriations through Global Affairs Canada rather than on an endowment — a structure that makes it unlike any private foundation in the corpus, closer in character to a bilateral aid agency with an unusually research-focused and civil-society-oriented mandate.

IDRC's relevance to the AI-good movement is not incidental: the Centre has built one of the most coherent public programmes in the world for ensuring that Global South actors — researchers, civil society organisations, grassroots technology groups, and communities — shape AI development rather than receive it as a fait accompli. Within the corpus, IDRC appears as a recurring funder of digital rights organisations and participatory technology projects across Africa, Latin America, Southeast Asia, and South Asia.

## AI for Development programme

IDRC's flagship AI-specific programme, **Artificial Intelligence for Development (AI4D)**, launched in 2020 as _AI4D Africa_, a [CAD 20 million partnership](https://idrc-crdi.ca/en/initiative/artificial-intelligence-development) between IDRC and the Swedish International Development Cooperation Agency (Sida) that funded nearly 100 locally-led innovations across the continent, established multidisciplinary AI laboratories at public universities, and created policy research hubs including one French-language and two English-language hubs. In 2025 the programme entered a second phase — _AI4D: Responsible AI, Empowering People_ — as a CAD 100 million-plus partnership between IDRC and the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO), with additional support from Germany's BMZ, the Gates Foundation, and Community Jameel, [spanning Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and the Pacific](https://www.ai4d.ai/about). The programme's four pillars — AI policy and governance, foundational research capacity, applied innovation, and scaling — are organized around "Southern Leadership" as a cross-cutting priority.

One of AI4D's most visible civil society grantees in the corpus is [Masakhane](../organizations/org-masakhane-nlp.md). Through a [CAD 5.36 million, 37-month grant](https://idrc-crdi.ca/en/what-we-do/projects-we-support/project/masakhane-ai-languages-hub) under the IDRC–FCDO AI4D partnership, IDRC funds the Masakhane AI Languages Hub in Kilifi, Kenya — the formal nonprofit structure through which the pan-African Masakhane community builds open-source, participatory, multimodal AI datasets across 40 African languages, making African language speakers co-producers rather than passive subjects of AI training pipelines.

## Global Index on Responsible AI and the FAIR Network

Beyond AI4D, IDRC leads two complementary programmes that extend its reach into governance and feminist dimensions of the AI-good space. The [Global Index on Responsible AI](https://idrc-crdi.ca/en/news/idrc-and-gac-join-leading-southern-researchers-build-global-index-responsible-ai) is a CAD 3.3 million-plus initiative, co-funded by Canada and USAID, that supports Research ICT Africa — a South African think tank — to produce a human-rights-based AI responsibility benchmark across 138 countries, anchored in the Global South and designed to feed directly into international AI governance debates. Separately, IDRC's **Feminist AI Research (FAIR) Network** convenes scientists, economists, and activists to identify and correct biases in AI systems, advancing AI tools designed from feminist principles.

## Digital rights grantmaking across the corpus

IDRC's reach into the movement's civil society infrastructure is visible across multiple corpus entries. [APC](../organizations/org-apc.md) (Association for Progressive Communications), the global network of more than 70 digital rights organisations, names IDRC as a current institutional funder alongside the Ford Foundation, Luminate, and the Oak Foundation — a relationship that gives IDRC direct leverage over one of the primary sub-granting and capacity-building vehicles for Global South digital rights work. [Sinar Project](../organizations/org-sinar-project.md), Malaysia's civic-tech anchor organisation, received [IDRC grants of USD 51,100 (2020) and USD 56,000 (2019)](https://sinarproject.org/media/about/funding) for legislative data and open-data hub coordination. [Fundación Vía Libre](../organizations/org-fundacion-via-libre.md), the Argentine free-culture and AI-audit organisation, lists IDRC as a funder channelled through Derechos Digitales and the fAIr Network. [Privacy International](../organizations/org-privacy-international.md) names IDRC among its [historic institutional funders](https://privacyinternational.org/about/financials). And IDRC contributed CAD 332,300 as a supporting funder of [INDELA](../funders/fund-indela.md), the Latin America digital rights re-granting fund that supported 20 projects across 22 organisations in 10 countries over 2019–2022.

## Significance in the movement's resource map

IDRC occupies a distinctive position among the corpus's funders: it is the only public funder in the corpus that has built an explicit, sustained, and well-capitalised programme — over CAD 120 million committed across its AI cycle — for ensuring that Global South communities are co-producers of AI, not just its subjects. Its grantmaking surfaces across the corpus precisely where the movement is thinnest: African-language AI development, Latin American digital rights infrastructure, Southeast Asian civic tech. That pattern reflects a funder operating at the under-tended frontier of the global AI-good movement rather than in the well-capitalised US and European NGO sector.
