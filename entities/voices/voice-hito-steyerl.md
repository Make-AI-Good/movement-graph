# Hito Steyerl

Hito Steyerl is the German filmmaker, moving-image artist, and critical theorist whose practice and prolific essay writing constitute the AI-critical-art field's most influential European strand — the Frankfurt School-inflected materialist analysis of what machine learning does to images, labor, and social visibility, delivered through video-essay installations at Tate Modern, MoMA, Venice Biennale, and Fondazione Prada, and through essays in *New Left Review*, *e-flux*, and *ArtReview* that have circulated as widely as any art-world critical text on AI (see [Person entry](../persons/person-hito-steyerl.md)). She is tracked here as a Voice because her sustained named public output — ["Mean Images"](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii140/articles/hito-steyerl-mean-images) (*New Left Review*, 2023) naming AI training as large-scale data theft; the ["Subprime Images"](https://criticalai.art/hito.html) keynote for the Critical AI in the Art Museum initiative (ANU, 2023); [*Medium Hot: Images in the Age of Heat*](https://www.versobooks.com/products/3329-medium-hot) (Verso, 2025); and a string of video-essay installations from *How Not to Be Seen* (2013) through *Mechanical Kurds* (2025) — collectively engages non-AI publics with AI critique and has been taken up as framing in creator-rights and AI-critical advocacy.

## Voice anchor

Steyerl anchors the **continental-Europe/Germany AI-critical-artist register** — the corpus's voice slot for an artist whose practice delivers AI critique through the essay-film and critical-theory traditions, engaging museum-going and general-intellectual publics with the political economy of machine learning.

The existing corpus voices in the AI-critical-artist register work primarily through investigative counter-surveillance disclosure and participatory dataset interventions: [Trevor Paglen](../persons/person-trevor-paglen.md) (*ImageNet Roulette*, counter-surveillance photography, the 600,000-image ImageNet removal); [Kate Crawford](../persons/person-kate-crawford.md) (*Atlas of AI*, the Knowing Machines Project, *Anatomy of an AI System*); [Stephanie Dinkins](../persons/person-stephanie-dinkins.md) (community-participatory AI platforms, Future Histories Studio). None occupies the register Steyerl anchors: the European essay-film artist producing Frankfurt School-inflected critiques of AI as part of a long investigation of media, capital, and power — the same critical-theory genealogy (Brecht, Adorno, Farocki) applied to neural networks, training datasets, and the global image economy.

## Core practice and AI-critical works

**The essay-film form.** Steyerl works in video installations that function as "essay films" — cinematic arguments assembled from footage, CGI, text, and voice-over, structured as critical essays rather than narratives. Her principal subjects since 2013 have increasingly been the systems by which images are made, classified, weaponized, and redistributed — rendering AI, surveillance infrastructure, and training-data labor visible as aesthetic and political problems simultaneously.

**"How Not to Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File" (2013).** Steyerl's best-known single work — [exhibited at MoMA and Serpentine Galleries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hito_Steyerl) — takes the form of a satirical instructional video on how to become invisible to machine vision, enumerating resolution targets, camouflage patterns, demographic categories, and algorithmic non-presence as survival strategies for the contemporary image environment. The work placed machine-vision infrastructure into general art-public discourse at the moment convolutional neural networks were entering production deployment.

**"Factory of the Sun" (2015).** Video installation tracing the extraction and conversion of digital labor — social-media content and gaming performance — into financial and military capital, screened as a mock-television production inside a motion-capture studio set. The work makes the infrastructure by which "free" digital activity is converted into data labor tangible for museum audiences.

**"Mean Images" (New Left Review 140/141, March–June 2023).** Steyerl's most widely cited written essay on AI image generation — [published in *New Left Review*](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii140/articles/hito-steyerl-mean-images) in its March–June 2023 double issue. The essay argues that machine-learning image generators produce not hallucinations but "statistical renderings" of averaged training data: images that are "mean" in the statistical sense (averages), the class-status sense (derived from the lowest-common-denominator of scraped web images), and the moral sense — built on what she terms "large-scale data theft" of artists' and photographers' work without compensation or consent. The framing circulated into creator-rights advocacy contexts and legal commentary on training-data consent.

**"Subprime Images" keynote, Critical AI in the Art Museum (ANU, October 2023).** Steyerl delivered the [opening keynote](https://criticalai.art/hito.html) for the Critical AI in the Art Museum series — an October–November 2023 initiative organized by the Computational Culture Lab at the Australian National University and explicitly aimed at helping cultural institutions critically engage with AI "rather than serving as an onboarding tool for Big Tech." The keynote applied her "subprime images" analysis to the institutional context of art museums being recruited as legitimating venues for AI companies.

**"Mechanical Kurds" (2025, MAK Vienna).** Video installation in [Steyerl's first solo exhibition at the Museum of Applied Arts Vienna](https://artreview.com/hito-steyerls-medium-hot-the-age-of-slop-artificial-intelligence-terry-nguyen-opinion/) (through January 11, 2026), in which precariously situated Kurdish refugees in Iraq are required to train the facial-recognition capacities of an AI — staging for museum audiences the global labor asymmetry through which marginalized populations provide unwaged training data for AI systems they have no access to or control over.

**"The Island" (Fondazione Prada, 2025).** [Solo exhibition at Fondazione Prada](https://artreview.com/hito-steyerls-medium-hot-the-age-of-slop-artificial-intelligence-terry-nguyen-opinion/) tying together AI, politics, and climate change — Steyerl's most recent large-scale institutional presentation of her AI and infrastructure critique.

**Medium Hot: Images in the Age of Heat (Verso, 2025).** [Eleven-essay collection](https://www.versobooks.com/products/3329-medium-hot) — published by Verso in May 2025 — addressing AI, large language models, algorithmic image creation, climate change, and labor. The collection argues against image production that "heats up the planet, disfranchises workers, and fuels the arms trade," examining topics including the exploitation of migrant populations as a global underclass of data proletariats, cryptocurrency mining in Kosovo, and Worldcoin's biometric data collection.

## Movement connections and public engagement

**Critical AI in the Art Museum (2023).** The [opening keynote](https://criticalai.art/hito.html) for ANU's Critical AI in the Art Museum series was explicitly a movement-infrastructure event: the program was designed to help cultural workers and art-museum staff "critically engage with AI rather than serve as an onboarding tool for Big Tech." Steyerl's "Subprime Images" keynote framed the institutional stakes — museums as potential legitimating venues for AI companies, and the obligation of cultural institutions to resist that recruitment — for an audience of museum professionals and cultural workers.

**On AI, Labor, and Power (New Museum, May 2026).** [Public program at the New Museum](https://www.newmuseum.org/event/05-28-26/) with [Trevor Paglen](../persons/person-trevor-paglen.md) — a direct pairing of two AI-critical artist voices in a labor-and-power framing for a public audience.

**Labor collective-action endorsement.** In commentary on *Medium Hot*, Steyerl [endorsed the 2023 WGA/SAG-AFTRA collective action](https://apollo-magazine.com/medium-hot-hito-steyerl-artificial-intelligence-review/) against tech monopolies as a powerful form of "reverse entropy" — workers wresting agency back from the systems accumulating it — framing creative-industry labor organizing as a model for resistance to AI-driven displacement.

## Why this is a Voice entry

A Voice entry is warranted — rather than additional structure on the [Person entry](../persons/person-hito-steyerl.md) — because Steyerl's public-facing output is itself the load-bearing object the corpus needs to track: the ["Mean Images"](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii140/articles/hito-steyerl-mean-images) essay installing "large-scale data theft" as a named framing in AI-critical and creator-rights advocacy discourse; the ["Subprime Images"](https://criticalai.art/hito.html) keynote at an explicitly movement-oriented initiative challenging cultural institutions to resist Big Tech recruitment; [*Medium Hot*](https://www.versobooks.com/products/3329-medium-hot)'s argument against AI practices that disfranchise workers and fuel the arms trade; and an exhibition practice from *How Not to Be Seen* (2013) through *Mechanical Kurds* (2025) that has made machine-vision, training-data labor, and AI surveillance visible to museum-going publics at major venues for more than a decade. The corpus's AI-critical artist voices — Paglen on surveillance infrastructure and participatory dataset critique, Crawford on extractive material costs and academic research-art, Dinkins on community-participatory platforms and Black-feminist AI justice — had no European voice anchoring the essay-film and critical-theory tradition that Steyerl represents.
