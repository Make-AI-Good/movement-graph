# Sam Gregory

Sam Gregory is the former Executive Director of [WITNESS](../organizations/org-witness.md) — a role he held for over 25 years, through August 2026 — and one of the movement's primary architects of civil-society-first content provenance and media authenticity infrastructure. He is tracked here as a Voice because his public output — the TED talk, the practitioner and academic writing, the congressional testimony, and the global framing work — constitutes the most sustained practitioner argument for why the make-AI-good movement must shape how media authenticity infrastructure is designed, not merely react to what platforms and standards bodies produce. Affiliation history and career detail are in the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-sam-gregory.md).

## Voice anchor

The Voice closes the **human-rights-documentation × AI media authenticity** slot. This is a specific nexus the corpus had open: the argument that synthetic media and deepfakes pose a distinctive and near-term threat to human rights defenders and frontline witnesses — not just the general public — because those communities depend on video documentation to prove atrocities, and deepfakes make genuine documentation deniable as much as they spread fake content. [WITNESS](../organizations/org-witness.md) was in the corpus as the organizational anchor for this slot; no Voice carried the principal practitioner-author of the argument driving that organization's AI-era strategy.

Two distinctions from adjacent voice slots in the corpus:

- **Distinct from the AI safety and existential risk register.** Gregory's domain is not catastrophic AI risk or AI alignment — it is the already-occurring harm to human rights documentation and evidentiary infrastructure. The deepfakes threat he addresses is operational and present: he has worked live cases with the Deepfakes Rapid Response Force and human rights groups in authoritarian contexts where video evidence has been dismissed as AI-generated. His framing is grounded in human rights practice theory and practitioner casework, not in speculative risk.

- **Distinct from the algorithmic accountability and surveillance register.** The voices anchoring facial recognition, data discrimination, and surveillance harms address how AI systems harm communities through classification, prediction, and profiling. Gregory addresses a different mechanism: how AI-generated synthetic media undermines the *epistemic infrastructure of witnessing* — the ability of the public, courts, and human rights bodies to establish what happened. The harm is to evidence and its credibility, not to subjects of automated decision systems.

## Signature framings

Three framings recur across Gregory's public output and constitute his distinctive contribution to the movement's vocabulary.

**"Prepare, Don't Panic."** The framing — launched as a 2018 initiative title and deployed consistently since — names a deliberate methodological posture: neither alarmism about deepfakes (which amplifies the liar's dividend by making people distrust everything) nor complacency (which leaves human rights defenders unprepared for the specific tactical challenges synthetic media creates). Preparation means training frontline documenters in provenance tools, building rapid-response forensics capacity before it is needed, establishing triage protocols before contested video arrives, and shaping technical standards and platform policies while the infrastructure is still being designed. The non-panic half is as load-bearing as the prepare half: Gregory has consistently argued that treating all potentially-deepfaked content as automatically suspect is a victory for bad actors, not a safety posture. [The initiative](https://www.gen-ai.witness.org/deepfakes-rapid-response-force/) grew from a framing paper into a global training programme and then into the Deepfakes Rapid Response Force.

**Provenance infrastructure, not detection.** Gregory's argument — most fully stated in ["Fortify the Truth"](https://academic.oup.com/jhrp/article-abstract/15/3/702/7261649) and the [TED Democracy talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/sam_gregory_when_ai_can_fake_reality_who_can_you_trust) — is that AI detection of deepfakes is structurally outpaced by AI generation, and the durable solution is cryptographic authentication at the moment of capture rather than computational forensics at the point of distribution. This is a design-philosophy claim with direct governance consequences: if provenance infrastructure (watermarking, cryptographic signing, chain-of-custody metadata) is the long-run answer, then the design decisions made inside standards bodies like the C2PA determine whether human rights defenders can use that infrastructure safely. Mandatory provenance that ties an image to its creator's identity may be privacy-preserving for a professional photojournalist and dangerous for an activist photographing a crackdown in an authoritarian state. The same infrastructure choice is protective in one context and potentially lethal in another — and the default designs circulating in corporate-led standards processes were designed for the former. Gregory's inside-C2PA work is aimed at changing those defaults before they harden into the universal architecture for media authenticity.

**The liar's dividend applied to human rights.** Building on the concept introduced in legal scholarship, Gregory has applied it directly to the human rights documentation context: deepfakes harm the movement not only by spreading false content but by providing bad actors with a ready-made defense — that any authentic video evidence of atrocities might be AI-generated. This asymmetric damage falls hardest on human rights defenders whose documentation of state violence is already contested by the accused, and for whom the additional credibility shadow of "maybe it's a deepfake" shifts the evidentiary burden further. The [2022 Journalism Practice paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14648849211060644) situates this asymmetry inside the broader misinformation-discourse ecosystem, arguing that frontline and distant witnessing are affected through different mechanisms and require different infrastructural responses.

## Public channels

Gregory's public output runs through four channels.

**TED Democracy and keynotes.** The talk ["When AI can fake reality, who can you trust?"](https://www.ted.com/talks/sam_gregory_when_ai_can_fake_reality_who_can_you_trust) (TED Democracy, November 2023, ~500,000 views) is Gregory's highest-reach public text — the 12-minute version of the WITNESS argument for a global audience: what happens to democracy when we can't trust what we see; what the three-track response looks like (detection, provenance, accountability); and why the human rights community has been working this problem longer than the platform-safety debate suggests. The 2025 Gruber Distinguished Lecture at Yale Law School ("Fortifying Truth, Trust and Evidence in the Face of Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technology") and keynote at the Palais Wilson for RightsX on Human Rights Day place Gregory in the institutional academic and intergovernmental registers alongside the mass-public one.

**Academic and practitioner writing.** ["Fortify the Truth"](https://academic.oup.com/jhrp/article-abstract/15/3/702/7261649) (*Journal of Human Rights Practice*, Vol. 15, Issue 3, 2023) is the field-facing practitioner argument — naming the specific design requirements authenticity infrastructure must meet to be useful rather than dangerous for frontline witnesses. The 2022 Journalism Practice article on deepfakes and authenticity infrastructure responses grounds the WITNESS approach in journalism studies and human rights practice theory. The 2024 PhD from the University of Westminster on participatory media, AI, and trust formalizes the practitioner work in academic theory of witnessing and digital media. An April 2026 IEEE Intelligent Systems publication documents the Microsoft–Northwestern–WITNESS Benchmark for Deepfake Detection — an interdisciplinary dataset for evaluating AI-generated audio, video, and image detection algorithms.

**Congressional testimony.** Gregory's [US House Oversight Committee testimony on "Advances in Deepfake Technology"](https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Sam-Gregory-House-Oversight-Committee-Advances-in-Deepfake-Technology-November-2023.pdf) (November 2023) brought the three-track framework into the US legislative record: detection infrastructure, content provenance standards, and accountability for platforms and AI providers generating and distributing synthetic media. Senate testimony extended this into upper-chamber deliberations on AI regulation. These appearances are the venue where Gregory's practitioner argument has most directly shaped US legislative framing on synthetic media.

**Standards work and institutional roles.** The C2PA Threats and Harms Task Force co-chair role is the venue with the highest governance leverage — inside the standards body whose decisions about mandatory identity disclosure, interoperability, and governance architecture will determine whether authentication infrastructure is usable by human rights defenders. The 2026 Scott Trust Board appointment (the trust that is sole shareholder of Guardian Media Group) places Gregory at the institutional intersection of independent journalism and AI-era media authenticity. The [2026 CAIDP AI Policy Leader Award for Civil Society](https://www.samgregory.info/about) marks the field's formal recognition of his policy-advocacy contribution.

The corpus carried [WITNESS](../organizations/org-witness.md) as the organizational anchor for the human-rights-documentation × AI media authenticity slot but no Voice carrying the principal practitioner-author of the argument that drove that organization's AI-era strategy. This entry closes the asymmetry — and fills the specific slot that no other corpus Voice currently occupies: the practitioner who insists that the human-rights community must be inside the room when authenticity infrastructure is designed, because who designs it determines whose safety it protects.
