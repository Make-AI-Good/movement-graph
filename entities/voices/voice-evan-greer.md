# Evan Greer

Evan Greer is Director of [Fight for the Future](../organizations/org-fight-for-the-future.md) (FFTF) and a queer trans musician and activist based in Boston. They are tracked here as a Voice because their public output — as campaign organizer, op-ed writer, and musician — constitutes the movement's primary articulation of how AI-powered surveillance intersects with LGBTQ rights and community-level harm. No other corpus Voice occupies this intersection while running large-scale consumer-facing campaigns and simultaneously deploying artistic production as a direct organizing tool. See the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-evan-greer.md) for affiliations and career history.

## Voice anchor

The specific slot Greer occupies is the **grassroots digital-rights campaign organizer who deploys art as a weapon** against surveillance normalization. Their public voice is inseparable from the campaigns they run at FFTF — the two registers, cultural production and political organizing, are deliberately fused rather than parallel.

The defining evidence of this function is the 2019 music-festival facial recognition campaign, run jointly with Tom Morello of Rage Against the Machine, which [won pledges from SXSW, Coachella, and Bonnaroo](https://findbiometrics.com/biometrics-news/tom-morello-evan-greer-rage-against-facial-recognition-machine-102503/) to ban facial recognition surveillance at their venues. The campaign ran alongside FFTF's July 2019 [federal ban campaign](../campaigns/camp-fight-for-the-future-ban-frt-us-cities-2019-ongoing.md), and Greer's framing of the technology — that facial recognition [poses "an existential threat to the future of human liberty"](https://www.fightforthefuture.org/news/2019-07-09-fight-for-the-future-launches-major-new-campaign/) and that "the debate over facial recognition will determine the fate of human society" — anchored the most confrontational wing of the US digital-rights movement's position: not regulation, but outright prohibition.

That position was elaborated in a [BuzzFeed News op-ed](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/evangreer/dont-regulate-facial-recognition-ban-it) titled "Don't Regulate Facial Recognition — Ban It," which became a reference point for the abolition-not-regulation argument in the US facial recognition policy debate. The op-ed argued that the harm from facial recognition surveillance is structural and inherent to the technology — not a misuse problem that better regulation can solve — a distinctly more confrontational stance than the "regulate with guardrails" position that most civil society organizations and all the tech companies themselves proposed.

## Music as organizing

The *[Spotify is Surveillance](https://evangreer.bandcamp.com/album/spotify-is-surveillance)* album (2021, Don Giovanni Records and Get Better Records; rated 6.7/10 by Pitchfork, featured by NPR and Rolling Stone) was deployed as a dual-register political act. Released simultaneously with the [StopSpotifySurveillance.org](https://www.stopspotifysurveillance.org/) campaign, it challenged Spotify's patent to analyze listeners' emotional states and demographic characteristics via voice — a technology Greer and FFTF framed as a concrete instance of AI-powered surveillance capitalism extending into intimate behavioural data. Campaign proceeds were directed to the Union of Musicians and Allied Workers, connecting the surveillance-capitalism critique to the labour-compensation dimension the UMAW was already pursuing separately.

*Spotify is Surveillance* established a deliberate pattern — an album whose title names a surveillance mechanism, released in tandem with a targeted petition campaign — continued with the *AMAB/ACAB* album ([September 2025](https://www.wbur.org/news/2025/09/04/evan-greer-amab-acab-new-album-newsletter), Get Better Records), which extended the scope from platform surveillance to trans rights, anti-fascism, and political solidarity, with features from Eve 6, Liz Berlin (Rusted Root), and Victoria Ruiz (Downtown Boys). The music-as-organizing model is Greer's distinctive contribution to the corpus: cultural artifacts that function simultaneously as movement messaging, fundraising, and coalition-building across audiences — the punk/indie music community and FFTF's campaign base — that do not otherwise overlap.

## Signature framings

Three framings constitute Greer's distinctive contribution to the movement's public argument.

**Surveillance capitalism as structural incompatibility.** Greer's diagnosis of AI surveillance harm does not rest on misuse or regulatory failure but on a structural claim: surveillance capitalism is "fundamentally incompatible with basic human rights and democracy." This positions the harm as inherent to the business model rather than a correctable externality, closing the door to industry-regulation frameworks that promise to allow the surveillance-driven model to continue under guardrails. Applied to AI, this analysis undergirds FFTF's consistent advocacy for structural interventions: outright bans on specific surveillance technologies, strong privacy legislation, antitrust enforcement, and what Greer calls "algorithmic justice legislation" — moving the conversation from AI safety standards to the power architecture of who controls AI systems and whose interests they serve.

**The LGBTQ-surveillance nexus.** Greer's work consistently surfaces the specific threat that AI surveillance poses to LGBTQ communities — a dimension that most US digital-rights advocacy treats as downstream of a universal privacy argument, but which Greer centres as primary. The KOSA opposition (2023–2024) is the clearest instance: while most critics of the Kids Online Safety Act focused on free-expression concerns, Greer's framing added a surveillance-accountability dimension — that the bill would empower state attorneys general to classify LGBTQ content as harmful, creating censorship and surveillance infrastructure targeting queer youth while doing nothing to address the data-exploitation model that actually harms children. The alternative Greer proposed — "strong privacy, antitrust, and algorithmic justice legislation to address the harms of Big Tech's monopoly power and surveillance-driven business model" — names the causal target that KOSA's content-moderation frame deliberately avoids. The same intersection shapes Greer's role on GLAAD's Social Media Safety Index advisory board and their analysis of how algorithmic content moderation specifically discriminates against LGBTQ content.

**Take the danger seriously; do not trust the industry.** Greer's [public statement on AI](https://www.commondreams.org/newswire/statement-dont-trust-the-ai-bros-but-take-the-danger-of-ai-seriously) articulated a left-digital-rights position that does not align cleanly with either the AI-safety movement or the mainstream "AI ethics" frame: "We can't trust the AI industry to regulate itself. We can't really trust anything these self-interested billionaires say. But we should take them seriously that the technology they are building is dangerous." This formulation separates the credibility claim about AI risk (which Greer accepts) from the institutional claim that AI labs and their leaders should guide the regulatory response (which Greer rejects). The alternative — listening to "independent experts, researchers, civil society, and affected communities" rather than AI executives — names the governance model that FFTF's campaigns embody: democratic accountability rooted in affected communities, not industry-convened safety frameworks. This position places Greer in a register distinct from both the institutional-pause movement (which engages AI labs as participants in the regulatory design) and the AI ethics academic framing — a campaign organizer insisting that the people most harmed by AI surveillance have the most legitimate claim to shape the response, and that technical expertise from inside the labs is not a substitute for democratic accountability from outside them.

## Public channels

Greer's public voice runs through four overlapping channels.

**Op-eds in mainstream and specialist outlets.** The Washington Post, Time, NBC News, Wired, The Guardian, Newsweek, and CNN carry Greer's regular bylined commentary on surveillance, digital rights, content moderation, and LGBTQ tech-policy issues. The TechPolicy.Press essay on [drawing a line in the tech accountability movement](https://www.techpolicy.press/evan-greer-asks-the-tech-accountability-movement-to-draw-a-line/) exemplifies the register: principled movement-strategy argument, directed at organizational allies, calling out the specific error of cozying up to anti-trans politicians for access. Greer's prescription — "technology built and controlled and governed by the communities that use and need them" — is the horizon against which they evaluate every incremental policy compromise.

**Fight for the Future campaigns.** FFTF's organizational infrastructure is Greer's primary venue for mass-scale action: the facial recognition ban campaign (2019–ongoing), the StopSpotifySurveillance.org campaign (2021), the KOSA opposition (2023–2024), and ongoing AI accountability and data-privacy work are all Greer-led or Greer-voiced efforts that have mobilized hundreds of thousands of people and generated sustained mainstream news coverage. These campaigns are the primary channel through which Greer's analytical framing has reached non-specialist publics at scale.

**Music and cultural production.** The *Spotify is Surveillance* and *AMAB/ACAB* albums are not a separate register from Greer's advocacy — they are a second channel for the same political argument, reaching the punk and indie music community and touring audiences who do not engage with digital-rights campaigns but who receive the political analysis embedded in the music. The 2019 music-festival campaign with Tom Morello demonstrated that this register can produce concrete campaign outcomes — not only cultural resonance.

**Expert commentary and advisory roles.** GLAAD Social Media Safety Index advisory board membership places Greer in the expert-consultation register for specific tech-policy assessments. Interviews across GBH, WBUR, Fast Company, and specialist media channels extend op-ed reach into audio and broadcast formats, building the Boston-area and national-public-radio audience that follows tech-policy issues through spoken rather than written media.

## Why this is a Voice entry

A Voice entry is created here, rather than additional structure on the [Person entry](../persons/person-evan-greer.md), because Greer's public-facing output is itself the load-bearing object the corpus needs to track: the BuzzFeed News op-ed establishing the "ban, don't regulate" argument for facial recognition; the 2019 music-festival campaign and its concrete venue pledges; the *Spotify is Surveillance* dual-register album-and-petition model; the KOSA framing connecting algorithmic harm and LGBTQ-specific vulnerability; and the "take the danger seriously, don't trust the industry" formulation that distinguishes a left-digital-rights AI position from both the AI-safety and AI-ethics mainstream frames. Without this entry, the corpus has no Voice anchoring the LGBTQ-digital-rights intersection — the specific position in the movement that insists affected communities, not industry or institutions, must lead the democratic accountability response to AI-powered surveillance.
