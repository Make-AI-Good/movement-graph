# Rashad Robinson

Rashad Robinson is a social justice strategist whose thirteen years as president of [Color of Change](../organizations/org-color-of-change.md) (2011–2024) made him one of the most consequential architects of the tech-accountability civil-rights movement in the United States. He is tracked here as a Voice because his public output — a foundational essay on narrative power circulated widely across the progressive movement sector, the Stop Hate for Profit campaign as a movement-strategy case, Congressional testimony on algorithmic accountability, and his 2026 book — constitutes the practitioner-facing argument for why racial justice movements must pursue structural leverage over platforms and corporations, not merely public visibility. Affiliation history and career detail are in the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-rashad-robinson.md).

## Voice anchor

The Voice closes the **US algorithmic-accountability × civil-rights civil-society** slot. The corpus carried [Color of Change](../organizations/org-color-of-change.md) as the organizational anchor for this slot — the nation's largest online racial justice organization, the co-organizer of Stop Hate for Profit, and the driver of the Facebook Civil Rights Audit — but had no Voice carrying the principal strategist behind those campaigns and the intellectual framing that drove them. Two aspects of the slot are most clearly missing without a Robinson Voice entry: the theoretical articulation of the presence-vs-power problem (why winning public attention fails to produce structural change) and the advertiser-accountability model as a transferable movement strategy, not just an episode in Color of Change's history.

The register is distinct from adjacent corpus voices. Robinson's argument operates at the intersection of racial justice and technology accountability specifically from the civil-society organizing tradition — organizing power through member mobilization, coordinated economic pressure on advertisers and platforms, and narrative strategy — rather than from technical AI safety, academic algorithmic fairness, or surveillance law. The distinctiveness is the combination: the largest Black-led online civil-rights organization as the organizing base, the advertiser stack as the economic lever, and the narrative infrastructure framework as the theory of how cultural change connects to rule-change.

## Signature framings

**Presence vs. power.** Robinson's central intellectual contribution — most fully developed in *From Presence to Power* (2026) and foreshadowed in the 2018 narrative-power essay — is the distinction between presence (being seen, heard, covered, invited to the table) and power (holding the leverage that changes rules, decisions, and structures). The progressive movement, in Robinson's analysis, has systematically confused the two: wins in visibility (a viral campaign, a corporate diversity statement, an invitation to testify) are experienced as victories even when the underlying rules that cause harm remain unchanged. For tech accountability specifically, the problem is acute because platforms have become expert at providing presence — convening civil rights meetings, commissioning audits, publishing transparency reports — while retaining structural control over algorithmic decisions that determine which content is amplified, which accounts are disabled, and which communities bear the costs of system errors. Robinson's framing names why corporate accountability campaigns must be designed around leverage (the advertiser, the regulator, the legislative threat) rather than dialogue.

**Narrative infrastructure, not narrative communications.** The 2018 essay ["Changing Our Narrative About Narrative"](https://nonprofitquarterly.org/changing-our-narrative-about-narrative-the-infrastructure-required-for-building-narrative-power/) (*Nonprofit Quarterly*, Winter 2018) distinguishes narrative power — "the ability to change the norms and rules our society lives by" — from the communications infrastructure movements typically invest in: press releases, talking points, viral content, and media appearances. Communications infrastructure generates presence; narrative infrastructure generates durable cultural change. The three required investments Robinson names are: sustained dispersion over time (not campaigns but constant circulation of ideas across channels and communities), human networks as delivery mechanisms (trained, networked people as the vehicle for narrative motion, not top-down content), and brand narratives (trusted organizational and leadership personas that shape behavior more effectively than abstract issue frames). The essay is the most widely circulated practitioner articulation of this distinction in the progressive movement sector and is frequently cited in philanthropy, narrative strategy, and racial justice literature as the framework for why movement communications routinely produce visibility without structural power shifts.

**Organized Black communities can hold multibillion-dollar companies accountable.** Robinson's framing of the Facebook Civil Rights Audit — "organized Black communities can hold multibillion-dollar companies accountable and pressure them to take definitive steps to end dangerous and discriminatory practices while setting new standards for the rules of engagement on their platform" — distills the strategic claim underlying Color of Change's corporate accountability approach. The audit, secured after years of sustained pressure by Color of Change and the Change the Terms coalition, was not a goodwill gesture: it was the product of escalating organized pressure through member mobilization, coalition coordination, and the credible threat of economic harm through advertiser boycott. The Stop Hate for Profit campaign, which followed in 2020, demonstrated the same logic at scale: converting a civil-society complaint about algorithmic amplification of hate into a procurement decision the platform's ad-sales team could not ignore. Robinson's public characterization of the Zuckerberg meeting — "a disappointment," with Facebook "showing up expecting an A for attendance" — established a language for what inadequate corporate accountability looks like and circulated widely as the frame for assessing subsequent platform responses to civil rights pressure.

## Public channels

Robinson's public output runs through four channels.

**Books and foundational essays.** *From Presence to Power: How to Take On the Fights That Matter and Win* ([One World/Penguin Random House, July 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/rashad-robinson-releases-from-presence-to-power-a-strategic-blueprint-for-winning-structural-change-302834504.html)) is Robinson's most complete articulation of the strategic framework he developed at Color of Change — a practitioner's guide to understanding how power is consolidated in tech, media, government, and corporate institutions and how social movements build leverage to rewrite the rules. ["Changing Our Narrative About Narrative"](https://nonprofitquarterly.org/changing-our-narrative-about-narrative-the-infrastructure-required-for-building-narrative-power/) (*Nonprofit Quarterly*, Winter 2018) is the foundational essay and the most circulated single text from Robinson's public output; it gave the progressive movement a practical language for distinguishing narrative power from communications infrastructure and is widely taught and cited in organizing, philanthropy, and racial justice contexts. Robinson also maintains a weekly newsletter, *How We Win*.

**Congressional testimony.** Robinson's [December 2021 testimony](https://colorofchange.org/press_release/rashad-robinson-testimony-at-house-energy-and-commerce-committee-hearing-on-holding-big-tech-accountable/) before the House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on Communications and Technology on "Holding Big Tech Accountable" is his primary legislative record — pressing for the Algorithmic Accountability Act, the American Data Privacy and Protection Act, the Fourth Amendment is Not for Sale Act, and the Digital Consumer Protection Commission Act, with specific demands that AI deployment in criminal justice, housing, and healthcare be conditioned on anti-discrimination safeguards. The testimony placed the civil-rights framing of algorithmic discrimination directly into the US legislative record in the period when the major platform accountability bills were being drafted.

**Media and public advocacy.** [Freedom Table](https://newsone.com/6583731/freedom-table-new-space-for-strategy-context-courage/) (NewsOne, 2025–) is Robinson's post-Color of Change public media vehicle — a monthly series described as "a show for builders, not bystanders," convening organizers, journalists, business leaders, and culture-makers on democracy protection, Black community power, and movement strategy. Episodes have addressed police violence normalization through crime television, attacks on DEI programs, and connected research to organizing tools. The series is co-produced through Rashad Robinson Advisors, the advisory entity Robinson founded following his departure from Color of Change in late 2024. The advisory practice focuses on narrative power strategy for foundations, nonprofits, and leaders navigating what Robinson describes as "a lopsided media ecosystem where opponents to progress maintain structural advantages."

**Platform accountability campaigns.** Stop Hate for Profit (2020), co-organized by Color of Change alongside the ADL, NAACP, [Free Press](../organizations/org-free-press.md), Common Sense Media, and Sleeping Giants, is the reference case for the advertiser-boycott movement strategy: translating a civil-society complaint about algorithmic racism into a coordinated spend withdrawal by 1,200+ advertisers. The Facebook Civil Rights Audit, separately secured after years of organized pressure by Color of Change and the Change the Terms coalition, produced findings on algorithmic amplification of hate, voter suppression content, and racial bias in content moderation — findings that grounded the advertiser-boycott demands in a document the platform had itself commissioned. Robinson also [issued public statements on the Biden AI Executive Order](https://colorofchange.org/blog/internal_press/in-light-of-president-bidens-executive-order-on-ai-rashad-robinson-president-of-color-of-change-issued-the-following-statement/) (October 2023) demanding enforcement mechanisms and legislative reinforcement, framing AI governance as an extension of the same civil rights enforcement struggle the organization had pursued against social media platforms.

## Why this is a Voice entry

A Voice entry is created here, rather than additional structure on the [Person entry](../persons/person-rashad-robinson.md), because Robinson's named public output is independently load-bearing for the corpus. The ["Changing Our Narrative About Narrative" essay](https://nonprofitquarterly.org/changing-our-narrative-about-narrative-the-infrastructure-required-for-building-narrative-power/) circulated as the practitioner framework for narrative power and infrastructure in the progressive sector and is cited across philanthropy, organizing, and racial justice literature far beyond Color of Change's own work. *From Presence to Power* (2026) is the systematic book-length articulation of the strategic theory underlying the tech-accountability civil-rights approach — published July 2026 and immediately placing his framework in the public record as the movement's theory-of-change text. Stop Hate for Profit is the movement-strategy case study most cited for the advertiser-boycott lever; Robinson's public framing of that campaign — naming Facebook's inadequate response, setting the terms for what real accountability requires — shaped how the campaign was interpreted and what it established as a transferable model. The corpus carried [Color of Change](../organizations/org-color-of-change.md) as the organizational anchor for the US algorithmic-accountability civil-rights slot; this entry closes the asymmetry by carrying the Voice whose public output constitutes the theory of change behind the organization's most consequential campaigns, stated in a form that the movement uses independently of any single organization.
