# Max Tegmark

Max Tegmark is co-founder and President of the [Future of Life Institute](https://futureoflife.org/) (FLI, 2014–) and Professor of Physics at MIT. He is tracked here as a Voice because his sustained public-voice work since 2014 constitutes the primary institutional architecture for the AI existential-risk and pause strand of the make-AI-good movement — not only articulating the risk argument but building the vessel through which that argument reaches mass audiences: the organisation that produced the movement's two defining mass-mobilisation open letters, the independent accountability mechanism that grades AI labs on safety practice, and the cross-aisle political coalition that extended the movement's reach beyond the technical community that originally carried it. See the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-max-tegmark.md) for affiliations and career history.

Where [Geoffrey Hinton](../persons/person-geoffrey-hinton.md) provides technical-founder credibility — the "godfather of AI" whose insider authority changes what can be dismissed — Tegmark provides institutional architecture: running the organisation that translates that credibility into coordinated public-pressure campaigns, regulatory proposals, and accountability structures. Where [Meredith Whittaker](../persons/person-meredith-whittaker.md) diagnoses AI as inseparable from surveillance-capitalism's business model and prescribes structural political action (privacy legislation, antitrust, workplace organizing), Tegmark anchors the safety/halt strand and prescribes regulatory precaution (FDA-style safety standards, superintelligence moratoria, independent oversight). Both reach the same policy implication — market incentives cannot produce safety without external intervention — but from categorically different angles.

## Voice anchor

The **movement-architect and institutional-infrastructure slot** that Tegmark occupies is structurally distinct from every other voice in the corpus. Advocacy campaigns, open letters, and accountability indices require an institutional vessel to produce them; Tegmark built and runs that vessel. FLI's stated mission — to "steer transformative technologies away from extreme, large-scale risks and towards benefiting life" — is both the organisation's mandate and the umbrella under which his entire public-voice arc has operated: funding AI safety research, producing policy-relevant open letters, developing accountability mechanisms, and building the cross-political coalition that extends the movement's reach.

The [Pause Giant AI Experiments](../messages/msg-pause-giant-ai-experiments.md) open letter (22 March 2023) is the primary instantiation of this architect function. Tegmark organised through FLI a letter that gathered 31,810 signatures — including Yoshua Bengio, Stuart Russell, Elon Musk, Steve Wozniak, and Yuval Noah Harari — calling for all AI labs to immediately pause for at least six months the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4. The letter's reach was not primarily technical: it moved the Overton window in mainstream journalism and politics, giving public expression to concerns that Tegmark had [documented privately among tech executives](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/oct/26/ai-artificial-intelligence-investment-boom) but that the competitive structure of the industry prevented them from expressing alone. The Pause letter was followed in March 2026 by the [Pro-Human AI Declaration](../messages/msg-pro-human-ai-declaration.md) — a structurally more ambitious document: a bipartisan coalition spanning from Steve Bannon and retired Joint Chiefs Chairman Mike Mullen on one side to Yoshua Bengio, Meredith Whittaker, Stuart Russell, and Daron Acemoglu on the other; from the AFL-CIO Tech Institute and American Federation of Teachers to faith coalitions and national-security figures. The Declaration's five pillars — keeping humans in charge, preventing power concentration, protecting the human experience, defending individual liberty, and establishing corporate accountability — and its outright prohibition on superintelligence development without scientific safety consensus represent the broadest political coalition the AI existential-risk movement has assembled.

No other corpus voice both runs the institutional infrastructure producing these documents and carries the ongoing public-voice record explaining why they are necessary.

## Signature framings

Four framings constitute Tegmark's distinctive contribution to the movement's public argument since 2014.

- **The prisoner's dilemma: no company can pause alone.** Tegmark's most politically consequential framing is not a statement of technical fact but a structural diagnosis of competitive dysfunction: AI labs are individually trapped in a race that makes safety-first behaviour impossible without external coordination. As he [told the Guardian in October 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/oct/26/ai-artificial-intelligence-investment-boom): "I felt that privately a lot of corporate leaders I talked to wanted [a pause] but they were trapped in this race to the bottom against each other. So no company can pause alone." And: "We're witnessing a race to the bottom that must be stopped. We urgently need AI safety standards, so that this transforms into a race to the top." The prisoner's-dilemma frame reframes the safety problem as a collective-action problem — one that markets cannot solve internally and that only external regulation (safety standards applied uniformly across labs) can resolve. The framing is politically consequential because it deflates the counter-argument that responsible labs are already managing safety internally: if competitive pressure prevents individual companies from prioritising safety even when their leaders want to, voluntary commitments are structurally unreliable. It also provided the movement with its clearest answer to the question of why prominent signatories like Elon Musk signed the Pause letter while simultaneously founding xAI: Musk supports a pause "just like a lot of other AI leaders. But as long as there isn't one, he feels he has to also stay in the game."

- **The FDA analogy as the regulatory-design template.** Tegmark's [consistent prescription for AI governance](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/09/26/1080299/six-months-on-from-the-pause-letter/) invokes the pharmaceutical regulatory model: "You never have to worry that some drug company is going to release some other drug that causes massive harm before people have figured out how to make it safe, because the FDA won't allow them to release anything until it's safe enough." The analogy carries a specific structural claim: the FDA imposes a proof-of-safety requirement *before* products reach the market, reversing the tech industry's default of ship-and-fix-later. Applied to AI, this implies that powerful AI systems should not be deployed until an independent body has verified safety properties — relocating the question from "are AI companies trustworthy?" to "what structural oversight exists independent of company incentives?" The analogy also answers the "we need to move fast" counter-argument: the pharmaceutical industry has continued to produce life-saving drugs at scale under FDA regulation; analogous oversight would not stop beneficial AI development, only constrain its deployment timing. As he [put it at the Pro-Human AI Declaration launch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/07/a-roadmap-for-ai-if-anyone-will-listen/): "If some creepy old man is texting an 11-year-old pretending to be a young girl and trying to persuade this boy to commit suicide, the guy can go to jail for that. We already have laws. It's illegal. So why is it different if a machine does it?" — extending the analogy from pre-market safety requirements to criminal liability for AI-caused harm.

- **Life 3.0 and the categorical-discontinuity argument.** Tegmark's 2017 book *[Life 3.0: Being Human in the Age of Artificial Intelligence](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780525558620/)* introduced a three-stage classification that anchors his entire public argument for why AI risk is categorically different from prior technology risk. Life 1.0 (bacteria) has both hardware and software — body and behaviour — determined by evolution. Life 2.0 (humans) can redesign its software (culture, language, skills) but not its hardware. Life 3.0 can redesign both — an AI that can modify its own code, architecture, and substrate, breaking free of every evolutionary constraint. The Life 3.0 framing closes the escape route that technology optimists typically rely on: that human ingenuity will always adapt to and domesticate new technologies. If a Life 3.0 system can redesign itself faster than humans can study it, the adaptation window closes. This is the conceptual architecture that makes his timelines non-trivial rather than alarmist, and the foundation on which his institutional prescriptions — a halt, safety standards, independent oversight — rest. The framework was introduced seven years before the mass-mobilisation phase of the AI safety movement, giving Tegmark's advocacy a first-mover claim that distinguishes his voice from later entrants: the 2014 co-authored op-ed with Stephen Hawking, Stuart Russell, and Frank Wilczek in a mainstream outlet was the first instance of this voice reaching beyond the specialist AI-risk community.

- **The bipartisan / across-the-spectrum coalition strategy.** The Pro-Human AI Declaration's coalition — [described by Tegmark](https://x.com/tegmark/status/2029180772477775986) as "from Bannon to Bengio, from unions to faith groups, from parents to NatSec leaders" — reflects a deliberate movement strategy: AI existential risk must reach past the technically literate and politically aligned who already agree, into the broader public that AI policy must ultimately answer to. Tegmark [cited polling at launch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/07/a-roadmap-for-ai-if-anyone-will-listen/) showing "95% of all Americans oppose an unregulated race to superintelligence" — deploying the coalition logic institutionally: a public-opinion majority spanning political parties provides the regulatory constituency that technical arguments alone cannot. The Declaration's drafting process included multiple in-person gatherings and a ratification meeting in New Orleans in January 2026, treating political breadth as a design criterion for the document itself, not just for its distribution. The coalition-building methodology is an evolution of the Pause letter's approach: where the Pause letter reached across institutional boundaries (research, tech industry, entertainment, civil society) through a co-signature model, the Declaration reached across ideological ones, establishing that the AI safety/halt argument can attract support from figures whose agreement on almost nothing else is documented.

## Public output and venues

Tegmark's public-voice work runs through five channels.

- **FLI-produced open letters and declarations.** The [Pause Giant AI Experiments](../messages/msg-pause-giant-ai-experiments.md) letter (March 2023) and the [Pro-Human AI Declaration](../messages/msg-pro-human-ai-declaration.md) (March 2026) are the movement's two defining mass-mobilisation documents and Tegmark's highest-reach public output. Each engaged non-technical publics at scale: the Pause letter generated global mainstream-news coverage within days of publication; the Declaration extended that reach into explicitly partisan and institutional channels — unions, faith groups, defence figures — that scientific open letters rarely access. These documents are the primary way Tegmark's voice has reached beyond AI specialists.

- **FLI AI Safety Index.** Launched in 2025 and updated biannually, the Index grades major AI labs — including Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, and others — on safety practice across six domains using 37 indicators assessed by an independent expert panel. The [Summer 2026 edition](https://futureoflife.org/ai-safety-index-summer-2026/) found that no lab achieved an A grade, with Anthropic leading (C+) and xAI and DeepSeek receiving F grades. The Index operationalises Tegmark's prisoner's-dilemma argument: by publishing comparative grades on safety practice, it makes visible the gap between claimed and demonstrated safety commitments across all labs simultaneously, creating the shared-information condition that voluntary commitments structurally cannot.

- **The mainstream press and interview circuit.** The [Guardian October 2023 interview](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/oct/26/ai-artificial-intelligence-investment-boom) anchors the race-to-the-bottom framing; the [MIT Technology Review September 2023 analysis](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/09/26/1080299/six-months-on-from-the-pause-letter/) his post-Pause-letter assessment; and the [2026 TechCrunch podcast](https://techcrunch.com/podcast/the-ai-safety-showdown-max-tegmark-on-government-anthropic-and-whats-next/) his engagement with US government policy. Lex Fridman Podcast appearances in 2018, 2021, and 2023 reach the technically literate audience that bridges AI research and AI policy. A November 2024 public characterisation of the AGI development race as "a suicide race" extended his vocabulary from competitive-dysfunction framing to extinction-stakes framing, tightening his register with the extinction-probability arguments that Hinton has anchored from a different direction.

- **Twitter/X (@tegmark).** An active commentary register carrying real-time announcements (the Pro-Human AI Declaration coalition launch), commentary on specific legislative votes ("The Senate just voted 99-1 to drop the OpenAI/Google plan to ban state AI regulation for a decade"), and public pressure on AI labs to match public commitments with internal behaviour. The channel's register is consistently direct: pressure on AI safety researchers inside frontier labs to "openly criticize your companies for pushing this shit" when their employers lobby against safety regulation. It functions as the primary channel through which Tegmark's public voice reaches AI researchers directly, without the mediation of news reporting.

- **The 2014 first-mover record.** The Hawking–Tegmark–Wilczek–Russell op-ed (2014), placing AI existential risk in mainstream scientific discourse before the current wave of AI safety attention, establishes Tegmark as a decade-long public voice on this question. This chronological first-mover position gives his 2023–2026 advocacy a record that differentiates his voice from later entrants to the AI safety public debate and grounds the "I've been saying this for ten years" credibility claim that he can make and they cannot.

## Organisational vehicles

Tegmark's public output runs almost entirely through the **Future of Life Institute** — the Cambridge, Massachusetts-based nonprofit he co-founded in 2014 with Jaan Tallinn (Skype co-founder), Anthony Aguirre, Viktoriya Krakovna, and Meia Chita-Tegmark, with $10 million in early funding from Elon Musk (2015) and subsequent major philanthropic backing. FLI's mission and Tegmark's Voice are structurally co-extensive: the organisation is the institutional form of his public argument, and his public argument is the primary driver of FLI's programme. The Pause letter, the AI Safety Index, and the Pro-Human AI Declaration are all FLI-produced; FLI is the primary infrastructure-scale vehicle for the AI existential-risk movement's open-letter and accountability-mechanism activities.

His MIT professorship functions as institutional credibility — confirming that his advocacy comes from a working scientist at a top research university — and as a channel for technical research (including Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks for interpretable AI, published 2024) that bridges his scientific and advocacy registers. TIME Magazine named him one of the [100 Most Influential People in AI in 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Tegmark), anchoring the international-public-influence register.
