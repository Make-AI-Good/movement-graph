# Abeba Birhane

Abeba Birhane is a cognitive scientist and Assistant Professor of Artificial Intelligence at Trinity College Dublin, where she is founder and Principal Investigator of the [AI Accountability Lab](https://aial.ie/). She is Senior Advisor in AI Accountability at [Mozilla Foundation](../organizations/org-mozilla-foundation.md), following a 15-month Mozilla Senior Fellowship in Trustworthy AI. She is tracked here as a Voice because her public output — the relational ethics framework, the dataset audit methodology that prompted MIT to remove the 80 Million Tiny Images dataset, the coining of "algorithmic colonialism," and the hate-scaling-laws research — constitutes the African-diaspora critical-AI intellectual contribution to the movement: a body of work whose framings circulate in AI-ethics scholarship, civil-society networks, and policy bodies independently of any single institutional affiliation. Affiliation history and career detail are in the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-abeba-birhane.md).

The combination of peer-reviewed academic output, direct outcomes (the MIT dataset removal), and policy-advisory roles (UN, OECD, Ireland's national AI advisory council) is the distinctive configuration.

## Signature framings

**Relational ethics as the alternative AI ethics foundation.** "Algorithmic injustice: a relational ethics approach" ([published in Patterns, February 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33659914/); an earlier version won the best paper award at the NeurIPS Black in AI workshop in 2019) is Birhane's foundational theoretical contribution. The paper reframes the question: mainstream AI ethics, she argues, grounds its analysis in abstract liberal principles — individual rights, procedural fairness, utilitarian welfare maximisation — and then asks which of these principles an algorithm violates. Relational ethics starts differently, from the position of those most systematically harmed: from the concrete embodied experience of the people at the margins of algorithmic systems, whose vulnerability is not an edge case but the site where the ethical stakes are actually visible. A relational approach holds that persons are constituted through relations of interdependence rather than as pre-social atomic individuals — an insight Birhane traces through African relational philosophy and embodied cognitive science simultaneously. The practical implication is a different accountability demand: not "does this algorithm comply with abstract fairness criteria?" but "whose vulnerability does this concretely harm, and what does their position reveal about the system's actual effects?"

**Dataset auditing as accountability methodology.** In 2020, Birhane and Vinay Uday Prabhu published [a paper at FAccT 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16923) examining the large-scale image datasets that power computer vision AI — ImageNet and the 80 Million Tiny Images dataset — and found racist and misogynistic labels and slurs embedded throughout. The paper's most consequential outcome was concrete: MIT voluntarily removed the 80 Million Tiny Images dataset from circulation, the first large-scale training dataset withdrawal driven by a civil-society audit rather than by a corporate decision. This established systematic algorithmic auditing as an accountability methodology with demonstrable effects — proof that examining what goes into AI systems, rather than only what comes out, could produce institutional responses. The same methodology extended to a saliency-cropping audit revealing that Twitter's, Apple's, and Google's photo-cropping algorithms consistently favoured white individuals over Black individuals — a finding published as the automated image-cropping systems were in widespread deployment. The audit work found its largest application in the [LAION training datasets](https://aial.ie/): the AI Accountability Lab's examination of LAION-400M and LAION-2B-en, foundational datasets for large generative models including Stable Diffusion, found hateful content embedded at scale — a finding that extended from content identification to the hate-scaling-law research establishing that larger datasets amplify rather than dilute the representation of racial bias and hate content, directly challenging the industry assumption that training data problems are solved by collecting more data.

**"Algorithmic colonialism."** In a [July 2019 essay in Real Life magazine](https://reallifemag.com/the-algorithmic-colonization-of-africa/) and a [peer-reviewed paper in SCRIPTed (2020)](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/script-ed/article/view/11592), Birhane coined the term "algorithmic colonialism" to name the structural pattern by which Western technology corporations impose AI systems on African populations following the three-act logic of colonial appropriation: justification (framing AI products as development solutions for the Global South), extraction (treating African populations as a data-rich resource while keeping the algorithms and value flows in the Global North), and institutionalisation (embedding Western algorithmic assumptions into African social infrastructure without local adaptation, creating long-term dependency). The framing's canonical short form — "colonialism in the age of Artificial Intelligence takes the form of state-of-the-art algorithms and AI driven solutions to social problems" — distinguishes algorithmic colonialism from the adjacent [data colonialism](../messages/msg-data-colonialism.md) framing used by Latin American civil society: where data colonialism addresses the general asymmetry of data accumulation, algorithmic colonialism names the AI-specific and Africa-specific structure of how that asymmetry plays out. The framing is covered in depth at [msg-algorithmic-colonialism](../messages/msg-algorithmic-colonialism.md); the Voice entry carries the author and connects the framing to her broader body of work.

**Scale amplifies, not dilutes, bias.** The hate-scaling-law research is Birhane's most direct intervention against the AI industry's dominant operational assumption. The industry's response to training data quality problems — racist labels, misogynistic content, hate speech — has been to collect more data, on the premise that at sufficient scale, problematic content becomes statistically marginal. The hate-scaling research quantifies the falsity of this premise: expanding the training dataset correlated with increased representation of harmful content; models trained on the expanded LAION-2B-en data were measurably more likely to produce racially biased associations than models trained on LAION-400M. The finding reframes the data-quality problem as one of curation and accountability rather than quantity — a methodological argument with direct policy implications for how foundation model developers manage training data.

## Public channels

Birhane's output flows through three intersecting channels. **Academic publications** — in venues including Patterns (Cell Press), FAccT, NeurIPS, and IEEE SaTML — carry the formal peer-reviewed contributions and reach AI researchers, AI ethics scholars, and computer science audiences who engage with the corpus of algorithmic accountability work. **Policy advisory roles** extend the academic work into governance: as a member of the UN Secretary-General's High-level Advisory Body on AI, the OECD AI Expert Group on Risk and Accountability, and Ireland's national Artificial Intelligence Advisory Council, Birhane translates the relational ethics framework and auditing methodology into regulatory and advisory contexts. **The AI Accountability Lab** at Trinity College Dublin is her institutional base for both the auditing work and for training the next generation of researchers in accountability-oriented AI research; it functions as a research group whose outputs are intended to influence both academic and public-facing debates about AI governance.
