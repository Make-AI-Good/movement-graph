# Alondra Nelson

Alondra Nelson is Harold F. Linder Professor at the Institute for Advanced Study and director of its Science, Technology, and Social Values Lab, tracked here as a Voice because her public output since 2022 constitutes the corpus's anchor for the US Black AI governance public-intellectual register: the architect function (Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights), the US representative to the UN AI governance advisory body, the co-founding of the AI Democracy Projects, and a sustained public intellectual output in Foreign Affairs, Science, and book-length form that operationalizes a rights-based, democratically-grounded vocabulary for AI accountability. Affiliations and career history are in the linked [Person entry](../persons/person-alondra-nelson.md).

Her register is distinct from adjacent corpus voices. [Timnit Gebru](../persons/person-timnit-gebru.md) anchors the algorithmic-harms/structural-discrimination register through DAIR's research program; [Meredith Whittaker](../persons/person-meredith-whittaker.md) the AI-as-surveillance-capitalism register through Signal and the AI Now Institute; [Joy Buolamwini](../persons/person-joy-buolamwini.md) the computational fairness register through Gender Shades and the Algorithmic Justice League. Nelson anchors the *policy-architecture-as-civil-rights* register: the figure who translates the movement's accountability and rights claims into government frameworks, international governance mechanisms, and a democratic-process vocabulary for civil society — a mode that the other algorithmic-accountability voices do not occupy.

## Voice anchor

The Voice closes the **US Black AI governance public-intellectual** gap in the corpus. The Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights is the US federal government's most visible AI rights framework, developed with direct civil society input and used as a reference point by state-level AI legislation, civil society organizations, and international governance bodies. Without a Nelson entry, the corpus has the organizations that adopted or built on the Blueprint framework, but not the architect who created the framework through a participatory public process and who continued to articulate its principles in international governance and public-intellectual venues after leaving government.

Her post-OSTP work closes a secondary scope question the inbox item flagged: the SCOPE-EDGE concern about whether her movement-shaping function would persist after leaving government. It has — across the UN AI Advisory Body, the AI Democracy Projects civil-society election-AI testing program, the Center for American Progress fellowship, and a public essay output in flagship venues (Foreign Affairs, Science) that continued the AI rights and accountability frame she built at OSTP.

## Signature contributions

**The Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights.** On October 4, 2022, Nelson's OSTP [published the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/ostp/news-updates/2022/10/04/blueprint-for-an-ai-bill-of-rightsa-vision-for-protecting-our-civil-rights-in-the-algorithmic-age/) — the first US federal framework articulating rights-based principles for automated systems. The Blueprint was developed through a year-long process engaging "workers, business associations, software engineers, researchers, civil society organizations and community activists, CEOs and entrepreneurs, and public servants across federal agencies" — one of the largest civil-society consultation processes for AI governance the US government had mounted. Its five protections — safe and effective systems, algorithmic discrimination protections, data privacy, notice and explanation, and human alternatives and fallback — provided a rights vocabulary for civil society organizations advocating on algorithmic accountability, and its framework has since been cited in state AI legislation, adopted in institutional policies, and incorporated into the advocacy language of AI accountability organizations. The Blueprint's influence registers not primarily through direct legal enforcement (it is non-binding) but through its function as a shared reference point for the movement: a US-government-endorsed articulation that algorithmic systems implicate civil rights, that people deserve human alternatives and recourse, and that the language of rights (not merely the language of risk) is appropriate for AI governance.

**UN High-Level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence.** In October 2023, UN Secretary-General António Guterres [appointed Nelson](https://www.ias.edu/news/un-high-level-advisory-body) to the UN High-Level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence as the United States representative — one of the first multilateral AI governance bodies convened at the UN level. The body's mandate includes building global scientific consensus on AI risks, developing options for the international governance of AI, and strengthening cooperation for AI and the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Nelson's appointment, arriving six months after her OSTP departure, marked the continuation of her governance function from the national to the international level, extending the rights-based frame she built in the US Blueprint into multilateral AI governance deliberations.

**AI Democracy Projects.** In early 2024, Nelson co-founded the AI Democracy Projects with journalist Julia Angwin of Proof News, conducting [the first publicly available testing](https://www.ias.edu/news/ai-chatbots-found-inaccurate-answering-voter-queries) of how leading AI models — OpenAI GPT-4, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, and others — respond to election-related queries. The testing engaged state and local election officials, journalists, and AI and elections experts in rating the accuracy of AI responses to voter questions. The finding: all leading AI models were unreliable, and therefore unsafe, for voters seeking election information — including demonstrably incorrect answers about state laws governing polling-place conduct. The Axios coverage carried the finding to mainstream political media under Nelson's name as former OSTP director, amplifying the warning in the pre-election period. The AI Democracy Projects exemplify the connective function Nelson occupies between government (OSTP), civil society (election officials, journalists), and the accountability movement: a testing infrastructure engaging non-AI-insider publics (election officials, voters) in evaluating AI systems' fitness for civic use.

**"The Right Way to Regulate AI" and the public-intellectual arc.** In January 2024, Nelson published ["The Right Way to Regulate AI"](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/right-way-regulate-artificial-intelligence-alondra-nelson) in Foreign Affairs — articulating the affirmative democratic case for AI governance and arguing for a focus on AI's public possibilities rather than dwelling solely on perils. The Foreign Affairs venue positioned her argument in the US foreign-policy establishment; the framing — rights-based, democratically grounded, attentive to the distributional question of who AI benefits — carried the Blueprint's underlying orientation into post-government advocacy. In Science, her essay "A Civic Grammar for AI Rights" proposed a vocabulary for contesting algorithmic power across different political contexts, offering civil society organizations a framework for AI rights claims. These essays represent the transition from government architect (building the Blueprint) to public intellectual (translating it for civil society and international audiences), with her IAS base as the platform.

**Auditing AI.** In April 2026, Nelson co-authored [Auditing AI](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262051729/auditing-ai/) (MIT Press Essential Knowledge series, published open-access) as a member of the Marquand House Collective — a multi-author collaborative including Marc Aidinoff, Christian Sandvig, J. Nathan Matias, Karrie Karahalios, Esha Bhandari, and others. The book provides a practitioner-oriented account of what AI auditing is, how to conduct it, and how to act on audit results, illustrated through canonical cases including AI-powered drone targeting, facial recognition misidentification, and algorithmic hiring bias. Published open-access to maximize civil society reach, it became a reference text for AI accountability practitioners outside technical research communities.

## Why this is a Voice entry

A Voice entry is created here, rather than additional structure on the [Person entry](../persons/person-alondra-nelson.md), because Nelson's public output since 2022 is itself the load-bearing object the corpus needs to track: the Blueprint as the founding moment of the US AI rights framework; the UN Advisory Body appointment as the continuation of the governance function to the multilateral level; the AI Democracy Projects as the civil-society election-AI testing architecture; and the Foreign Affairs / Science public-intellectual output as the ongoing articulation of the rights-based AI governance vocabulary to policy and civil society audiences. The corpus's algorithmic-accountability register had entries for the research voices (Gebru, Buolamwini), the platform-critique voice (Whittaker), and the computational-fairness voice — but not the policy-architecture-as-civil-rights voice: the public intellectual who built the US government's rights framework through a participatory civil society process and then continued to translate it into multilateral governance and public-intellectual form. Without this entry, the Blueprint itself and its author's post-government movement function are absent from the corpus.
