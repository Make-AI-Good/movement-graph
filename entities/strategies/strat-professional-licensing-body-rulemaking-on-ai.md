# Professional-licensing-body rulemaking on AI in licensed practice

The movement engages the regulatory bodies that license credentialled professionals — state bars, state medical boards, boards of engineering, nursing boards, teacher-certification authorities, psychology and social-work boards — to issue binding rules on AI use inside the practices those bodies regulate. The output is a licensee-binding rule: a state bar ethics opinion on AI-generated pleadings, a medical board disclosure requirement for AI-diagnostic tools, a scope-of-practice ruling on AI-assisted triage, a licensure sanction attached to non-compliant AI adoption. The target is the licensure authority, not the AI vendor, and the enforcement instrument is the licence itself — a practitioner who violates the rule risks the practice ticket that makes their livelihood legal.

An actor chooses this because a licensure authority is an institution whose sanctions the AI-deployer cannot lobby around at the industry level. A state medical board's rule reaches every practitioner in that state regardless of the vendor's federal-lobbying spend, communications budget, or venture backing. The strategy also converts each individual practitioner from a passive consumer of the AI product into a licence-holder whose own professional survival depends on the deployment-time compliance the movement-shaped rule requires — a shift in enforcement locus from the deployer to the deploying practitioner that no other pressure lever in the corpus achieves at scale.

It trades off jurisdictional coverage and pace. Each state licensure body is its own venue — fifty state bars, fifty medical boards, fifty engineering boards, with parallel structures in Canadian provinces and EU member states — and rulemaking cycles run in years, not months. Licensing bodies are also structurally susceptible to industry capture through the very insurance carriers and continuing-education providers that fund their members' practice, and the rule only reaches licensees. It is structurally blind to the deployer directly and reaches none of the unlicensed sectors (software, retail, content generation, gig-economy work) where most AI deployment lives.

## Ecology

Sibling to [cross-professional refusal of AI integration into practice](strat-cross-professional-refusal-of-ai-integration.md): both operate through professional substrate, but the actor and instrument differ — professional refusal runs through the profession's own collective apparatus (union, guild, association) refusing AI directly, while licensing-body rulemaking runs through movement pressure on an external regulator that then imposes rules the individual licensee is bound by. The pair is often complementary: the same medical association that carries a refusal position in bargaining can simultaneously petition the state medical board for a licensure rule that binds the wider profession beyond union membership.

Sits alongside [mandatory algorithmic impact assessment](strat-mandatory-algorithmic-impact-assessment-requirement.md) and [consumer-facing AI use disclosure mandate](strat-consumer-facing-ai-use-disclosure-mandate.md) as one of the movement's regulatory-instrument strategies, but distinguishes itself by the *venue*: MAIA and disclosure mandates typically target legislatures or executive agencies, while licensing-body rulemaking targets the profession-specific regulator. The strategic advantage is that licensure bodies can issue binding rules on a much narrower administrative timeline than a legislature and without the AI-vendor lobbying access that concentrates at the legislative and federal-agency levels.
