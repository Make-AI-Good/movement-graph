# Community-owned cooperative AI infrastructure

The movement stands up cooperative and community-owned AI infrastructure from below — worker-owned model-training labs, community-owned inference cooperatives, data-cooperatives whose contributors own the training-data pool they contribute to, member-owned deployment consortia serving cooperative-federation members, university-and-union joint compute pools, worker-consumer hybrids that pair cooperative producers with the communities they serve. The strategy names cooperative production of AI as a movement-side alternative to commercial-vendor and state-provided AI both. The vehicles are the cooperative bar's existing legal forms (worker cooperatives, consumer cooperatives, cooperative corporations, mutual benefit corporations) adapted for AI-specific production questions: whose data trains the model, whose labour builds it, who governs it, who receives its outputs and revenues, and how it is licensed to those who use it. The reference precedents are the platform-cooperativism movement of the 2010s (Stocksy, Fairmondo, Up & Go), the Mondragon Corporation as the enduring worker-cooperative demonstration case at industrial scale, the Sardex cooperative currency network, and the credit-union / mutual-insurance / cooperative-utility lineages that carried the twentieth-century US cooperative movement.

An actor chooses this strategy because it addresses a demand no other strategy in the corpus does: the movement's own *infrastructural existence* independent of the commercial vendors it contests. Every strategy that campaigns *against* commercial AI (audit, refusal, litigation, regulation, boycott) operates on the vendor's terrain; a cooperative-production strategy operates on infrastructure the movement *owns*, refuses to make available for uses inconsistent with its charter, and hands over to member-communities as a demonstration that another AI is possible. The strategy is also **prefigurative**: it makes the movement's normative claims about how AI *should* be produced (worker voice in training decisions, community consent in data use, democratic governance of models, non-commercial licensing) legible in a working example, which is a specific rhetorical asset in framing and coalition work no policy demand alone provides. And it converts a cluster of the movement's other strategies — worker organising of supply-chain labour, indigenous data sovereignty, collective licensing — from *demands on external vendors* into *practices inside movement-owned production*.

It trades scale for ownership. A cooperatively-owned AI infrastructure serves its members and cooperative-federation partners at a scale orders of magnitude smaller than commercial-vendor deployment, cannot compete with the vendor bar on model performance without capital investment the cooperative sector does not have, and depends on a member base disciplined enough to prefer a slower and less capable model on movement-alignment grounds over a faster and better one from a commercial vendor. The strategy is also structurally slow: cooperatives take years to mature, cooperative federations take decades, and the movement-side patience required to wait out the trajectory is scarce in a movement whose urgency-frame demands wins today. The strategy is finally exposed to **commercial capture** — the cooperative-branded product that quietly runs on the same commercial infrastructure it claims to replace, the worker-cooperative that turns commercially competitive at the cost of its cooperative-governance discipline — and the movement's authority to certify which cooperative-branded AI is authentically cooperative is not yet built.

## Ecology

**Sibling to [public-option AI infrastructure](strat-public-option-ai-infrastructure.md), distinct in ownership register.** Both name movement-side alternatives to commercial-vendor AI; both operate on the infrastructural layer rather than as external campaigns. The distinguishing question is **who owns the resulting AI**: public-option AI is *state-provided public utility* — Library of Congress, Swiss federal government, EU public inference — with public-goods provision as the ownership register; cooperative AI is *member-owned cooperative production* — worker cooperatives, consumer cooperatives, data cooperatives — with mutualist ownership as the register. The two are complementary alternatives to commercial AI, not competing ones, and a mature movement infrastructure would carry both arms (public option for universal-service provision; cooperative for member-governance depth).

**Structurally downstream of [organise AI supply-chain workers](strat-organize-ai-supply-chain-workers.md) and [indigenous and community data sovereignty](strat-indigenous-and-community-data-sovereignty.md).** Cooperative production requires organised workers to constitute the worker-side ownership, and requires data-governance frameworks that recognise community consent over training data. A cooperative AI project standing up without either arm behind it defaults into the vendor-shape it claims to depart from — worker labour extracted without cooperative governance; training data drawn without community consent — and reproduces the very production relations the strategy is meant to demonstrate an alternative to.

**Contested by [parallel community research institution](strat-parallel-community-research-institution.md) on infrastructure-vs-research axis.** Research institutions produce knowledge about AI; cooperative-production institutions produce AI itself. A movement that runs only the research arm cedes the production layer entirely to commercial vendors and the state; a movement that runs only the cooperative-production arm has no external legitimacy the research arm provides. The two together are the emerging shape of movement-side infrastructure work, and the balance between them is itself a live strategic question the movement's cooperative and research funders are actively working through.
