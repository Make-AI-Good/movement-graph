# Community benefit agreements for AI infrastructure siting

Legally binding contracts between an AI-infrastructure developer (typically a hyperscale data-centre operator, a compute-power-plant sponsor, or a chip-fabrication host) and a community coalition in the siting jurisdiction, extracting enforceable commitments in exchange for community non-opposition to the project's zoning, water, and utility approvals. The typical CBA package covers water and electricity consumption caps enforceable against the operator's own metering, revenue-sharing with the host jurisdiction, local-hire and prevailing-wage clauses, a community veto or trigger for site expansion beyond the initial footprint, air-and-noise monitoring paid for by the operator with independent-third-party access to the data, and provisions on cooling-water discharge that go beyond the state permit floor. The strategy converts unavoidable siting decisions — projects the coalition cannot block outright — into structured extraction of terms that would otherwise be externalised onto the host community.

An actor chooses this strategy because outright blocking is available only where the local political opportunity structure retains real planning, utility, or environmental-review authority, and increasing numbers of US Sun-Belt jurisdictions, the UK's proposed "AI Growth Zones", and equivalent European "digital sovereignty zones" are deliberately stripping that authority to attract hyperscaler capital. In an environment where the plant will be built somewhere, the strategic question shifts from "block" to "extract" — and the CBA is the well-attested legal instrument the environmental-justice movement developed over three decades of losing outright siting fights on stadiums, ports, chemical plants, and prisons. The strategy also builds durable local organising infrastructure regardless of the specific project outcome: the coalition assembled to negotiate a CBA is available for the next facility, the next utility rate case, and the eventual county-level policy fight.

It trades absolute refusal for negotiated concession, and the specific concessions extracted can end up producing legibility on the host community's actual costs the developer would rather have kept implicit. A CBA also legitimises the project in a way outright opposition does not — the coalition's signature on a binding agreement makes it harder to reopen the fight on subsequent expansions or to challenge the parent project in court. The strategy depends on the developer's assessment that the CBA is cheaper than the litigation delay and reputation cost of continued opposition, which requires the coalition to hold a credible threat of that delay and cost across the negotiation period. And CBAs are enforceable only to the extent the community coalition has the legal infrastructure to enforce them years after the plant goes live — a five-year monitoring commitment is worth what the coalition's five-year enforcement capacity turns out to be.

## Ecology

**Sister to [local community resistance to AI data-centre buildout](strat-local-fight-against-ai-data-centers.md).** The two strategies are the negotiating table and the picket line of the same siting fight. A campaign runs outright opposition until it becomes clear the project will proceed anyway; the coalition then pivots to CBA-negotiation, using the opposition's credibility as leverage for terms. The two strategies are structurally paired — a coalition without a credible opposition posture cannot extract meaningful CBA terms, and a coalition running only opposition leaves the extraction option on the table when the fight is lost.

**Complements [ratepayer opposition to AI compute utility subsidies](strat-ratepayer-opposition-to-ai-compute-utility-subsidies.md).** Where opposition focuses on the utility rate structure the plant runs on, the CBA focuses on the plant's direct obligations to the host community. The two lanes address different accountability nodes — utility commission and county planning board — of the same siting decision.

**Cross-movement precedent, well-attested.** The reference lineage is the Los Angeles Alliance for a New Economy's 2001 Staples Center CBA, the model instrument that generalised through the Community Benefits Law Center, the Partnership for Working Families / Jobs to Move America, and the environmental-justice movement's response to petrochemical siting in Louisiana's Cancer Alley, Gulf Coast port expansions, and Bronx bulk-diesel facilities. In each case the movement had lost the underlying siting decision and used the CBA to extract terms the facility developer would otherwise have externalised entirely. AI data-centre siting is at exactly the point in its buildout curve where the environmental-justice movement pivoted from blocking to negotiating; the strategic transposition is well-precedented.

**The AI-movement adopter is not yet identified.** MediaJustice's regional organising base in the US South and Kairos Fellowship's data-centre training infrastructure could be the substrate a CBA campaign runs on, but the corpus's current data-centre entries describe opposition rather than negotiated concession. Whether the movement chooses to open a CBA lane or holds to outright opposition is a strategic-posture decision the corpus does not yet have evidence of the movement making.
