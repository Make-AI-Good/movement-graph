# Transnational solidarity organising across jurisdictions on AI

Movement work coordinated across national borders on the same AI vendor, system, or harm class: Kenyan content moderators supporting German content moderators supporting Colombian content moderators against the same BPO chain, a European data-rights coalition sharing legal filings and campaign framing with a Brazilian or South African counterpart against the same biometric vendor, coordinated campaigns in Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, and the UK against a shared cloud-provider's data-centre buildout, a global coalition speaking with one voice at UN General Assembly cycles on autonomous weapons. The vehicle is standing cross-border organising infrastructure — shared campaign calendars, translated messaging, coordinated actions timed to the same product launch or regulatory window, movement-to-movement staff exchanges between geographies, shared litigation-and-audit resources — not one-off international-solidarity statements or standalone hashtags.

An actor chooses this strategy because AI systems are transnational by construction. A single vendor deploys the same model in a hundred jurisdictions; a single hyperscaler builds data centres on four continents; a single training dataset is scraped from all the world's languages; a single content-moderation contract is subcontracted through BPOs across three continents with the workforce concentrated wherever labour is cheapest. The movements resisting these systems are structurally national — one country's parliament, one country's labour law, one country's data-protection regulator, one country's press. Coordinated cross-border pressure is the only mechanism that can pace a transnational vendor: a legislative win in one jurisdiction is bypassed by relocation, a national moratorium is undercut when a neighbour offers the incentive package, a boycott in one market is inconsequential when the same product ships globally. Transnational solidarity also lets movement work in one geography share evidence, tactics, and legal precedents with counterparts elsewhere — the Foxglove UK data-centre judicial-review shape carried to South Africa, the Amsterdam Uber rulings' evidentiary discipline carried to gig-worker cases in India, the Reclaim Your Face framing carried as a bargaining reference across the world's biometric-surveillance fights.

It trades speed for reach. Cross-border coordination absorbs enormous convening labour across time zones, languages, and political registers, and the coalition's shared analysis must span jurisdictions whose legal architectures, political opportunity structures, funding ecologies, and media environments diverge. The strategy is exposed to power asymmetries between Global North and Global South partners — funding, legal capacity, media reach, English-language dominance — that structurally centre Northern coalitions unless the transnational apparatus is deliberately designed to invert the flow, and coalitions that fail to invert it reproduce the colonial pattern in the movement's own operations. A coordinated cross-border campaign against a specific vendor can also be diluted when partners in one jurisdiction quietly cut a deal with the same vendor for regulatory relief, and the collective bargaining position weakens in the jurisdictions still holding the line.

## Ecology

Distinct from [cross-movement coalition bridging](strat-cross-movement-coalition-bridging.md), which is coordination between *different movements* in one geography (climate + AI, labour + AI, racial-justice + AI). This strategy is coordination *between the same movement's actors* across geographies — the same AI-good movement's Kenyan, German, and Colombian arms working the same content-moderation vendor together. The two strategies are complementary and often co-deployed by the same standing infrastructure: a transnational data-rights coalition also bridges cross-movement inside each of its member geographies.

Distinct from [coalition lobbying of binding regional regulation](strat-coalition-lobbying-of-binding-regional-regulation.md), which is a *within-region* legislative campaign — the EU AI Act, US federal AI legislation, national data-protection reform. This strategy is *across-region* coordination — the two are complementary, and a mature transnational coalition typically runs a within-region lobbying arm in each of its participant jurisdictions on aligned but jurisdictionally-adapted asks.

Distinct from [multilateral treaty process engagement on AI](strat-multilateral-treaty-process-engagement-on-ai.md) and [humanitarian disarmament treaty](strat-humanitarian-disarmament-treaty.md). Those strategies engage a specific *supra-national body* — the Council of Europe, UNESCO, the CCW, the UN General Assembly — as the venue where the coalition's demand is negotiated. Transnational solidarity is a horizontal organising register across national civil-society coalitions, whether or not any multilateral body is involved; the coalition's vertical engagement with a treaty process is a distinct strategy the same coalition may or may not additionally run.

Feeds [organise AI supply-chain workers](strat-organize-ai-supply-chain-workers.md) as the container that lets a content-moderation workforce fragmented across geographies bargain collectively against a single BPO chain — an African Content Moderators Union making common cause with German content moderators and Colombian content moderators, with a Northern legal-defence organisation like Foxglove holding the litigation architecture, is the strategy at its most concentrated form. Feeds [map the AI supply chain of state harm](strat-map-the-ai-supply-chain-of-state-harm.md) as the political register the supply-chain map is subsequently mobilised in — a supply-chain investigation naming vendor, deployer, and financier in three jurisdictions requires cross-border coalition to convert the map into a campaign.

The strategy's structural bet is that cross-border coordination can pace transnational capital. Its structural exposure is that it can also *reproduce* transnational capital's centre-periphery geometry in the coalition's own operations unless funding, agenda-setting, and leadership are deliberately re-centred at the periphery — the same critical failure the [decolonial reframing] register warns of, applied to the movement's own operational shape rather than to the industry it fights.
