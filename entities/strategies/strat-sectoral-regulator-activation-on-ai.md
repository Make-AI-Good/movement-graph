# Sectoral regulator activation on AI

Civil-society organisations activate the *existing* stack of sectoral regulators — the Federal Trade Commission on consumer protection and deceptive practices, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission on hiring and workplace discrimination, the Department of Housing and Urban Development on fair housing, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau on credit and lending, the Food and Drug Administration on medical devices and software-as-medical-device, the Federal Communications Commission on telecommunications, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration on autonomous vehicles, national data-protection authorities under GDPR, sectoral competition authorities — to bring AI systems under jurisdictions those agencies *already* possess. The strategy's theory of change is that AI does not need a new regulator to be regulated: every consequential AI deployment is a hiring decision, a housing decision, a credit decision, a medical device, a common-carrier service, or a consumer product, and the regulator with jurisdiction over the underlying decision already has statutory authority over its algorithmic form. The movement's job is to activate that authority — file complaints citing existing statutes, submit rulemaking comments interpreting existing rules to cover AI systems, litigate against agencies that decline to exercise the authority they hold.

An actor chooses this strategy because the statutory groundwork is already done and the agency infrastructure — the investigatory divisions, the enforcement mechanisms, the settlement architecture, the private right of action — already exists. Waiting for Congress or Parliament to legislate a new AI-specific regulatory body forfeits the years or decades of enforcement the movement could win under existing authority in the interim, and any single sectoral regulator's decision to interpret its statute as covering AI ripples through the industry faster than a new statute ever could (the EEOC's 2023 technical assistance document on AI in hiring shifted vendor practice within months, before any new legislation touched the question). The strategy also converts the very fragmentation of the sectoral regulator stack from a bug into a feature: an AI vendor facing simultaneous EEOC action on hiring products, HUD action on tenant-screening products, CFPB action on credit-scoring products, and FTC action on marketing claims cannot mount a unified defence, and each favourable ruling in one sector supplies precedent the movement then imports into the next.

It trades unified coherence for fractured coverage. No single regulator holds jurisdiction over the whole of AI, and any given deployment class may sit in a gap between sectoral authorities (autonomous weapons between military and consumer product jurisdictions; generative AI content between speech and product regulations); such gaps are the openings where an AI-specific body advocacy has its strongest case. The strategy also depends on agency willingness to interpret existing authority to cover AI, which varies with administration and political cycle — the same FTC that pursued the ROBOCOP-esque HireVue matter under Chair Khan may decline to pursue an equivalent case under a successor. And the strategy competes for movement resources with the parallel strategy of pushing for new AI-specific bodies; the two are complementary in principle but rivalrous for coalitional time, funder attention, and hearing testimony bandwidth.

## Ecology

**Opposed in theory of change, complementary in practice, to [advocate for a new AI regulatory body](strat-advocate-for-a-new-ai-regulatory-body.md).** The new-body strategy holds that AI is categorically distinct from prior technology-regulation shapes and needs its own statutory framework; the sectoral-activation strategy holds that AI is a general-purpose technology inflecting decisions already regulated by domain agencies. In principle these can be pursued together — activate existing authority now, build a new body over the longer horizon — and mature coalitions typically pursue both. In practice the strategies pull differently on framing: sectoral activation frames AI as *hiring / housing / credit / medical / speech* problems the movement addresses one sector at a time; new-body framing consolidates the problem as one demanding a single agency. The framing choice shapes which coalitions the movement can build and which victories it can claim.

**[Mass participation in agency rulemaking comments](strat-mass-participation-in-agency-rulemaking-comments.md) is a primary tactic of this strategy.** Sectoral rulemakings on AI — EEOC on employment discrimination, CFPB on credit-decisioning, HUD on tenant screening, FDA on adaptive-algorithm devices — are the openings where mass public comment infrastructure supplies the strategy's political weight. The tactic is used by other strategies too, but sectoral-regulator activation is where its highest-leverage applications land.

**[Data protection authority complaint machinery](strat-data-protection-authority-complaint-machinery.md) is the EU-form of this strategy.** European coalitions running strategic Article 22 GDPR complaints, Article 15 access-rights actions, and DPA enforcement priorities are running sectoral-regulator activation on the specific sectoral regulator (the data-protection authority) that GDPR grants broad AI-adjacent jurisdiction to. The tactic is a national-variant of the family this strategy names.

**Fed by [empirical audit and expose](strat-empirical-audit-and-expose.md).** Sectoral regulator complaints run on documented harm evidence — the audit that surfaced disparate-impact patterns in hiring AI, the investigation that traced tenant-screening false positives, the study that measured discriminatory lending in AI-underwritten credit — and the audit-and-expose infrastructure the movement already runs is the primary evidence pipeline the sectoral-activation strategy converts into enforcement matter.

**Structurally paired with [strategic personnel placement in AI regulatory agencies](strat-strategic-personnel-placement-in-ai-regulatory-agencies.md).** The activation strategy needs agency staff willing to receive a complaint as a matter to be investigated rather than as a filing to be shelved; the placement strategy is the organising infrastructure that engineers those staff. A sectoral-activation campaign against a captured agency staffed with industry alumni produces a paper trail and no enforcement; the two arms compound.
