# Movement-run consumer-facing AI certification

Movement organisations design, own, and administer a consumer-facing certification mark — a visible label a product or service can carry, granted by the movement's certifying body to products that meet a published set of criteria (no AI-training on scraped copyrighted work, no biometric surveillance modality, no autonomous-decision-making on named consequential categories, verified provenance of training data). The vehicle is a small independent certifying organisation with a defined criteria document, a verification procedure, an appeals process, and licensing agreements with certified products; the model is Fair Trade, USDA Organic, B Corp, Cruelty Free International's Leaping Bunny, the FSC forest-certification mark. The strategy converts individual purchasing decisions into an aggregated market-signalling mechanism the movement itself controls.

An actor chooses this strategy because a mark on the product at point-of-purchase is a substantially different consumer-intervention than a boycott list or an accountability index — it operates positively (steering purchase toward compliant products) rather than negatively (steering purchase away from non-compliant), it lives on the packaging the consumer already looks at, and it accretes market share among producers who compete to earn the mark. The strategy also produces a *movement-owned normative artifact* — the criteria document itself — that becomes reference material for adjacent regulation, procurement standards, and litigation, and it converts the movement's abstract policy demands ("no non-consensual training data") into a specific operationalisable definition the movement's own certifying body defends. Adjacent-movement precedent shows that mature certification marks can move billions of dollars of consumer spend at the aggregate; a well-designed AI-domain mark could plausibly do the same.

It trades speed of impact for methodological sustainability. Certification marks take years to become recognisable to consumers — Fair Trade needed a decade to reach broad consumer awareness — and the verification apparatus is expensive to operate: audits of AI systems' training data, deployment modalities, and compliance requires specialised staff and imposes verification costs on producers that price out small compliant producers. The strategy is also vulnerable to mark-dilution: as adoption scales, industry-adjacent competing marks proliferate (industry-friendly "responsible AI" seals with lower thresholds), and the consumer signal is muddied unless the movement mark can outcompete for cultural authority. And the technical challenge of verifying AI-training-data provenance and AI-behavioural compliance in a way an auditor can attest to is unsolved at scale — the certification's substantive integrity depends on verification methods that do not yet exist for the more ambitious criteria a movement mark would want to certify against.

## Ecology

**Distinct from [community-defined benchmarks and standards](strat-community-defined-benchmarks-and-standards.md).** Benchmarks are movement-authored *technical measurement instruments* used by researchers, journalists, and regulators to evaluate AI systems' performance. Certification marks are movement-authored *consumer-facing labels* attached to products that pass criteria. Benchmarks produce scores that inform expert readers; marks produce trust signals that inform consumer readers. The two can share underlying methodology — a mark's criteria may reference a benchmark's threshold — but their audience and communication register differ.

**Distinct from [standing AI accountability index](strat-standing-ai-accountability-index.md).** An accountability index ranks a fixed roster of large targets comparatively. A certification mark is granted individually to any product meeting the criteria and is not comparative — a certified product is not "more certified" than another certified product, and the mark's audience is consumers making individual purchase decisions rather than journalists or investors comparing corporate actors.

**Distinct from [consumer-facing AI use disclosure mandate](strat-consumer-facing-ai-use-disclosure-mandate.md).** Disclosure is a *statutory obligation* to reveal AI use, universal to the product class covered, enforced by government. Certification is a *voluntary market signal* of compliance with movement criteria, granted by the movement's certifying body, sought by producers who see market advantage in bearing it. The two are the negative and positive consumer-informational surfaces: disclosure tells the consumer what to worry about, certification tells the consumer what to trust.

**Feeds [organized consumer boycott of AI products](strat-organized-consumer-boycott-of-ai-products.md).** A consumer-boycott strategy that also nominates a certified alternative is more effective than one that only names a target to refuse — consumers respond to positive substitution more readily than to categorical refusal. A mature certification mark provides the boycott campaign with a "buy this instead" list the campaign can direct consumers to.
