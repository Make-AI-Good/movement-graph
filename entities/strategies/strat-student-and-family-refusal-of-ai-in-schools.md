# Student and family refusal of AI in schools

Students, parents, and family associations organise against specific AI deployments in the schools and universities they are on the receiving end of — algorithmic proctoring, AI grading, predictive-discipline scoring, AI-driven advising, chatbot mental-health services, AI-integrated LMS surveillance — as the *served constituency* rather than as the profession delivering the service. The vehicle is student walkout and social-media campaign at the university level, parent-teacher-association pressure and school-board testimony in K-12, coordinated opt-out campaigns, class-action complaints under student-privacy statutes, and coalition demand-letters signed by hundreds or thousands of the students the deployment is targeted at. Reference precedents include the algorithmic-proctoring backlash of 2020–2021 (student petitions at Miami University, Baylor, University of Illinois, Australian National University; university-by-university opt-out or cancellation of Proctorio, ProctorU, and Honorlock), the parent-organised opposition to district-level algorithmic teacher-evaluation and student-scoring pilots, and the long ed-tech-refusal lineage of opt-out testing movements and student-data-privacy organising (Parent Coalition for Student Privacy, Class Size Matters).

An actor chooses this strategy because the professional-refusal instrument does not reach the deployments whose harm accrues to the students themselves rather than to the profession: a district can procure an algorithmic-proctoring system over a faculty senate's objection, an admissions office can deploy AI advising without consulting the counselors, a residence-life office can install biometric attendance without the professoriate's involvement. In each case the constituency the harm falls on — students, parents, families — has no seat inside the professional bodies whose refusal strategy operates on the delivery side, but does have institutional standing inside the school or university's own governance apparatus (student government, elected school boards, PTAs, university trustees) that no external actor can substitute for. The strategy operates in the classic captive-audience posture that ed-tech has always had to answer to: the student cannot exit the classroom, and organised refusal is the only leverage available inside a compulsory institution.

It trades sustained infrastructure for episodic force. A student walkout produces a media moment and often extracts a concession on a specific tool, but the underlying procurement pipeline resets each year with new incoming cohorts and new AI products; the strategy is chronically re-fought without the institutional memory the professional-refusal arm has. Parent-organised K-12 opposition faces the coordinated-organising ceiling of the school-district geography — a district-by-district fight is a serial ground war the AI vendor's sales team is structurally better equipped for than an ad-hoc parent coalition. And the strategy is exposed to the strongest counter-move ed-tech has always used: framing student and parent objections as anti-innovation, technology-averse, or standing in the way of the students' own advancement, a frame the vendor's marketing to school boards deploys as its first line.

## Ecology

**Distinct from [cross-professional refusal of AI integration](strat-cross-professional-refusal-of-ai-integration.md).** That strategy is the profession — the teachers, nurses, defenders, engineers — refusing to integrate AI into their own practice, from within the professional-authority ground the profession stands on. This strategy is the served constituency — the students, patients, defendants, users — refusing the AI deployed on them, from within the school or hospital or agency's user-side governance. The refusal registers are different (professional expertise vs. served-community standing), the institutional levers are different (contract clauses and licensure vs. student government and school boards), and the same-target campaigns typically need both arms to move a district or university: teachers refusing to administer, and students and families refusing to be administered to.

**Distinct from [AI literacy curriculum organizing in schools and libraries](strat-ai-literacy-curriculum-organizing-in-schools-and-libraries.md).** That strategy shapes what students *learn about* AI. This strategy refuses what schools *deploy on* students. The two often live inside the same educator-and-parent coalition but address opposite objects — curriculum shapes the students' understanding of AI in the world; refusal contests the schools' use of AI on the students themselves. A district can adopt a strong AI-literacy curriculum while also deploying algorithmic proctoring on the same students, and vice versa.

**Distinct from [institutional procurement refusal of AI vendors](strat-institutional-procurement-refusal-of-ai-vendors.md).** Procurement refusal is a *buyer-side* institutional decision made by the school or university's procurement office in response to internal pressure. This strategy is the *pressure* — the student and parent coalition organising to move that procurement decision. The strategies are sequential: procurement refusal is the outcome the family-refusal campaign is trying to produce, and the family arm is the political substrate the procurement office answers to when a decision is contested.

**Fed by [counter-narrative framing](strat-counter-narrative-framing.md).** The vendor's dominant marketing frame in the schools register is that AI serves the student — better proctoring produces fairer testing, AI advising democratises college access, biometric attendance improves safety. A refusal campaign cannot land without a counter-frame that reads the same tools as surveillance, deskilling, and privatisation of what the school owes the student directly. The ed-tech-critical lineage from Audrey Watters onward is the framing substrate this strategy runs on.

**Sibling of [organized consumer boycott of AI products](strat-organized-consumer-boycott-of-ai-products.md)** but structurally distinct at the exit condition. A consumer boycott of an AI product is available because the consumer can decline to purchase; a student in a compulsory-attendance school and a parent whose only accessible school is the one deploying the tool have no exit and cannot rely on the boycott's revenue-side logic. The strategy accordingly runs on institutional-governance pressure (school boards, trustees, elected officials) rather than on the market-side pressure a boycott produces.
