# Movement-anchored AI-harm oral-history archive

Durable, publicly accessible documentary infrastructure — long-form recorded testimony, transcribed accounts, and curated collections — capturing first-person narratives from people harmed by AI systems: content moderators traumatised by extreme-content review, benefit-claimants wrongly denied by algorithmic decisions, families of children harmed by generative-AI companions, workers displaced or algorithmically managed, communities living near data-centre buildout, professionals whose livelihoods were absorbed by AI outputs. The archive is not evidentiary in the immediate litigation sense — that is [survivor-led testimony as evidence](strat-survivor-led-testimony-as-evidence.md)'s register — but *memorial*: a permanent record of what the movement documented at the moment the harms were unfolding, held by an institution with a mandate to keep it available across the political cycles the current fight will not resolve.

An actor chooses this strategy because the harms an AI-good movement is contesting are being produced faster than any evidentiary or regulatory record can absorb them, and because the political cycles the fight runs on will erase the individual accounts by the time institutional actors are willing to hear them. The reference precedents — the AIDS Memorial Quilt and ACT UP's oral-history archives, the Shoah Foundation's videotaped survivor testimonies, the Nunca Más truth-commission collections from Argentina and Chile, the StoryCorps 9/11 collection — all worked the same double register: an immediate documentary practice for the community that produced the testimony, and a durable public record the next political moment would be forced to reckon with. The strategy also builds an anchor for cultural production the movement will need decades from now: the writers, filmmakers, educators, and researchers who eventually process the current period will draw on the archive rather than on scattered contemporaneous journalism.

It trades immediate operational leverage for long-horizon memory, and it depends on institutional custody the movement often does not have — university archives with real preservation budgets, museum collections with public-access commitments, or independent archives with sustained funding across ten or twenty years. The strategy also carries a genuine harm-of-participation risk: recording an account of one's own harm is an act of exposure, sometimes retraumatising, and the archive's duty of care to its subjects has to be structurally real rather than aspirational. Consent, redaction, embargo, and takedown protocols are load-bearing infrastructure the strategy runs on, not paperwork attached to it. And an archive is only as good as its findability and use — an archive nobody reads is an unfunded warehouse.

## Ecology

**Distinct from [public AI incident registry building](strat-public-ai-incident-registry-building.md).** The incident registry is a structured dataset — machine-readable, queryable, feeding audits and regulatory work. The oral-history archive is a first-person documentary corpus — long-form, human-readable, feeding cultural production and durable memory. The two strategies are complementary infrastructures of different registers; a mature movement runs both, and each requires different institutional custody and funding models.

**Distinct from [survivor-led testimony as evidence](strat-survivor-led-testimony-as-evidence.md).** That strategy places the survivor at the centre of a specific legal or regulatory campaign; the testimony is an evidentiary instrument aimed at a discrete institutional outcome. This strategy holds the testimony beyond any single campaign — its output is available to every future campaign and to actors outside the movement entirely. A witness testifying in the Motaung petition (a survivor-led-testimony instance) might separately record an oral-history interview whose value is durable and non-instrumental; the two roles complement each other but do not substitute.

**Feeds [popular culture shaping AI imagination](strat-popular-culture-shaping-ai-imagination.md)** and [counter-narrative framing](strat-counter-narrative-framing.md). The novelists, screenwriters, journalists, and educators who eventually shape the public imagination of the current AI moment will draw on the archive far more than on the specific advocacy campaigns it emerged from. The AIDS crisis's cultural reckoning ran on the ACT UP oral-history record two decades later; the AI moment's eventual cultural reckoning will run on the archives the movement builds now, or on their absence.

**The AI-movement adopter is not yet identified.** Fragments exist: the [DAIR Institute's](../organizations/org-dair-institute.md) research corpus documents specific harms; the [African Content Moderators Union's](../organizations/org-african-content-moderators-union.md) organising materials preserve testimony; [Data & Society](../organizations/org-data-and-society.md)'s research reports carry embedded interviews. What is not yet visible in the corpus is a *dedicated* oral-history archive with permanent institutional custody, an explicit accession policy, and a public access commitment beyond the sponsoring institution's own research programme.
