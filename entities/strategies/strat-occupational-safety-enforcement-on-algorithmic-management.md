# Occupational-safety-agency enforcement on algorithmic management

Movement organisations and worker coalitions organise the occupational-safety-and-health enforcement layer — the federal Occupational Safety and Health Administration and its state analogs in the US, the Health and Safety Executive and its European counterparts, the ILO's occupational-safety machinery — to bring their existing statutory authority against algorithmic-management practices that intensify workplace hazard: algorithmic pace-setting (Amazon warehouse quotas, gig-delivery time-guarantees, call-centre AHT enforcement), biometric worker surveillance (keystroke tracking, wearable-monitored posture and location), predictive-scheduling volatility, and automated discipline systems whose thresholds produce documented ergonomic-injury, mental-health, and traffic-safety consequences. The vehicle is inspection-request complaints, agency-referral coalition-letters, coordinated worker-side testimony at rulemaking hearings, and — where the statute reaches — dedicated organising for new occupational-safety standards on algorithmic-management practices (California AB 701's 2021 warehouse-quota transparency requirement is the clearest US precedent; the EU's ongoing Platform Work Directive on algorithmic-management transparency is the trans-national analog). Reference lineages include the Warehouse Worker Resource Center's OSHA-referral organising against Amazon fulfilment-centre injury rates, the National Employment Law Project's algorithmic-management-and-safety pipeline, the Athena Coalition's Amazon-warehouse safety-and-surveillance work, and the older ergonomic-standards battles OSHA fought through the 1990s and 2000s.

An actor chooses this strategy because the occupational-safety statute in most industrialised jurisdictions is already broad enough to reach algorithmic-management harms without new legislation — the Occupational Safety and Health Act's General Duty Clause requires an employer to furnish employment "free from recognised hazards … likely to cause death or serious physical harm," and an algorithmically-enforced pace producing documented repetitive-strain and traumatic-injury rates fits the recognised-hazard test on its face — and the strategy is accordingly enforcement-side rather than legislative-side, which means the movement's throughput does not depend on securing new statute against a lobbied opposition. The strategy also lands in an agency with existing constituency mobilisation infrastructure (union safety committees, injured-worker advocacy, standing OSHA-consultation cooperatives) that a fresh AI-regulatory-body strategy would need to build from scratch. And it produces documented findings — inspection reports, citations, settlement injunctions — that are legibly enforcement outcomes the movement can carry into procurement, litigation, and legislative pipelines as authoritative evidence rather than as movement-side claims.

It trades reach for the agency's own capacity limits. OSHA's inspection workforce has been chronically under-funded across the past four decades, and its citation authority is bounded by penalty ceilings that a large employer treats as a cost of doing business rather than as a deterrent; the strategy's real leverage is the *documentation* the inspection process produces more than the citation itself. The strategy is also structurally limited to the *workplace* deployment case — it does not reach algorithmic-management practices in the platform-worker register where the worker is legally classified as an independent contractor rather than an employee, and the AI-vendor's own workforce (data annotators, content moderators, ML engineers) is often outside the standard occupational-safety framing that manufacturing-and-warehouse work fits into cleanly. And the strategy is exposed to the political counter-move an incoming administration hostile to the agency can execute — leadership appointments, rulemaking-freeze directives, enforcement-discretion instructions — in ways the private-litigation and legislative arms are not.

## Ecology

**Distinct from [organise AI supply chain workers](strat-organize-ai-supply-chain-workers.md).** That strategy organises the workers themselves — data annotators, content moderators, gig-platform drivers — into unions and worker centres whose collective action is the primary force. This strategy organises the *enforcement agency* to act on the workers' behalf under existing safety statute. The two are structurally complementary: the worker organisation is the constituency the agency answers to and the source of the complaint intake the agency's inspections respond to; the agency's citations and settlements are the enforcement outputs that raise the cost of the practices the workers organised against. A mature labour-side response to algorithmic management runs both arms in the same shop.

**Distinct from [formal unionization of AI industry workers](strat-formal-unionization-of-ai-industry-workers.md).** Unionisation contests the AI industry's own workforce (Alphabet Workers Union, News Guild bargaining on AI provisions). This strategy contests the *deployed* algorithmic-management systems in workplaces the AI industry sells into — the warehouses, delivery fleets, call centres, and gig platforms whose workforce is downstream of the AI vendor's product. The unionisation arm shapes the AI industry's internal culture; the safety-agency arm limits what the AI industry's product is allowed to do to workers in other industries.

**Distinct from [strategic litigation against algorithmic state decisions](strat-strategic-litigation-against-algorithmic-state-decisions.md).** That strategy litigates *state* deployment of algorithmic decision-making (benefits denials, predictive policing, risk-scoring in courts). This strategy engages *private-employer* deployment of algorithmic management through the workplace-safety regulator. Both are enforcement-side against algorithmic-decision harm, but the targets, statutes, and agencies differ.

**Feeds [mandatory algorithmic impact assessment requirement](strat-mandatory-algorithmic-impact-assessment-requirement.md)** at the evidentiary layer. Inspection findings and citation records from safety-agency actions supply the injury-rate and hazard-recognition documentation an impact-assessment regime's substantive requirements can be built on and defended by; the safety-agency register produces the *evidence base* an impact-assessment statute then generalises into a proactive disclosure requirement.

**Paired with [mass participation in agency rulemaking comments](strat-mass-participation-in-agency-rulemaking-comments.md)** at the rulemaking-window register. Where the safety agency opens a rulemaking on algorithmic management (the OSHA advance-notice-of-proposed-rulemaking on heat-injury prevention has become the closest procedural analogue for how a comparable ANPRM on algorithmic pace would work), the mass-comment infrastructure supplies the record the agency writes its final rule on and the political cover the rule needs to survive judicial review. The two strategies share adopter substrate: the same worker-safety coalitions that submit inspection complaints also organise the rulemaking-comment mobilisation.
