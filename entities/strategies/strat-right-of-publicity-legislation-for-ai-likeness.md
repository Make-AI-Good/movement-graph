# Right-of-publicity legislation for AI-generated likeness

Legislation establishing a personal property right over one's voice and image against unauthorised AI replication — a "digital replica right" — at state or federal level. The strategy converts an existing common-law body (state right-of-publicity doctrine, most developed in California, New York, and Tennessee) into an explicit statutory floor covering AI-generated voice clones, face swaps, digital body doubles, and posthumous replicas. Recent US instances anchor the strategy: Tennessee's ELVIS Act (2024) as the first state-level statutory update explicitly reaching AI voice replicas; the federal NO FAKES Act's bipartisan legislative pipeline in the Senate and House; parallel state bills in New York, Illinois, and California updating existing statutes for generative AI.

An actor chooses this strategy because right-of-publicity doctrine is one of the few legal registers where individual, non-commercial plaintiffs already have a well-attested cause of action against corporate wrongdoers, and because the digital-replica frame reaches unauthorised generative output through a private property theory that does not require proving harm at the population scale copyright and civil-rights framings do. It is politically legible across the ideological spectrum — the property-rights argument, the parental-rights argument (deepfake child sexual abuse material), the working-class-labour argument (performers whose likenesses are the substance of their labour) each carry the same statutory demand. And unlike a general disclosure or provenance regime, the strategy delivers an enforceable individual right with private-action standing: the plaintiff is the person whose likeness was cloned, not a regulator with a discretionary docket.

It trades reach for legibility. Right-of-publicity is a private property right; it does not touch AI outputs that are not likenesses, does not reach diffuse harms (bias in decision systems, environmental costs, labour displacement), and can be used offensively by well-resourced defendants against journalists and satirists on the same statutory hook. First Amendment carve-outs are a genuine negotiation the movement has to run inside its own coalition — the union-and-industry-together shape of the NO FAKES Act coalition (SAG-AFTRA + RIAA + MPA + platforms) papered over serious disagreements about the scope of parody, biographical use, and news exceptions. The strategy also risks industry capture through licensing regimes: a right the platforms can price and clear at scale reallocates rent, but it does not necessarily reduce the volume of AI replica production. Whether a licensable right actually protects the underlying labour, or becomes the compliance layer for an industrialised replica economy, is the load-bearing empirical question the next five years of enforcement will answer.

## Ecology

**Complements [creator-class collective bargaining on generative AI](strat-creator-class-collective-bargaining-on-generative-ai.md).** The 2023 SAG-AFTRA strike settlement's digital-replica taxonomy (informed consent, compensation, control) is the vocabulary that ended up in the NO FAKES Act; the private-contract and public-statute tracks feed each other, with the contract negotiated first and the statute generalising the terms.

**Adjacent to [consumer-facing AI use disclosure mandate](strat-consumer-facing-ai-use-disclosure-mandate.md)** and [content provenance and cryptographic authenticity](strat-content-provenance-and-cryptographic-authenticity.md) — the disclosure and provenance regimes lower the discovery cost of a right-of-publicity violation. Without disclosure or provenance a plaintiff bears a much higher forensic burden proving a likeness was AI-generated; the two families are the enforcement infrastructure the private right runs on.

**Distinct from broader deepfake criminalisation** (see [strat-ai-specific-criminal-code-amendments](strat-ai-specific-criminal-code-amendments.md)) — right-of-publicity is a civil-law property right with a private plaintiff; criminal-code amendments run on public prosecution and target a narrower behaviour set (non-consensual intimate imagery, election interference). Movement coalitions running the right-of-publicity track often decline to carry the criminal-code track for coalition reasons, and vice versa.

**Cross-movement precedent.** The reference lineage is the Elvis Presley estate's twenty-year campaign for Tennessee's original 1984 right-of-publicity statute — the same statute the 2024 ELVIS Act updates — and the parallel California and New York statutory expansions that built the state-level floor generative AI is now testing. The right-of-publicity movement is one of the few in American law whose statutory home has always been the state, not the federal government, and the AI-era update inherits that federalism structure directly. The load-bearing risk is that the AI industry's federal-preemption push (see [preemption defense of subnational AI authority](strat-preemption-defense-of-subnational-ai-authority.md)) succeeds against exactly the state statutes this strategy has spent forty years building.
