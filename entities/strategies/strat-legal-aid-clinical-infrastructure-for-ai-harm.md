# Legal-aid and law-school-clinic infrastructure for AI harm

The movement invests in *clinical* legal capacity — law-school clinics, dedicated AI-harm sections inside legal-aid organisations, pro-bono panels at bar associations, medical-legal-partnership analogs, and specialist AI-harm hotlines — that represents *individual* people harmed by AI decisions in their *own* cases: a wrongful benefits denial from an algorithmic eligibility system, a wrongful arrest from a face-recognition match, a hiring rejection from a screening model, an eviction from a landlord-side AI tenant-scoring tool, a deepfake harassment incident, an insurance-denial pushed by an AI adjudicator. The vehicle is a clinic or dedicated section with intake infrastructure open to individuals, staff attorneys and student-attorneys with AI-technical competence, an AI-specific case-management infrastructure, and referral relationships with movement-side technical partners (audit organisations, expert witnesses, forensic technologists) the clinic can pull in on individual cases. The reference models are the Legal Aid Society's Housing Access to Justice work, the National Legal Aid & Defender Association's public-defender infrastructure, university law-school clinical education (Harvard Cyberlaw Clinic, Georgetown IPR, Berkeley Samuelson, NYU Technology Law and Policy), and the medical-legal-partnership movement in which legal aid embeds inside health-care institutions.

An actor chooses this strategy because it addresses the base rate: for every AI-harm incident that becomes a movement-shaped strategic case, hundreds or thousands of similarly-harmed people never get representation at all, and the *disparity* between the movement's high-visibility strategic-litigation wins and the everyday landscape of un-represented AI-harmed people is itself a movement-legitimacy question. The strategy is also **service-and-organising**: an individual client whose eviction, arrest, or benefits case the clinic wins becomes an organising contact, an evidentiary witness for legislative testimony, and a member of the constituency the movement's other strategies claim to represent — and the clinical intake pipeline surfaces the *pattern* of AI-harm in real time, feeding the movement's audit, litigation, and legislative arms with an evidence stream drawn from lived cases. And it produces a bench: law students who trained in an AI-harm clinic enter the profession with AI-technical case experience, staffing the private and public-interest bar with an incoming generation of AI-fluent lawyers the strategic-litigation and amicus arms will subsequently pull from.

It trades scale for depth per case. Individual representation is expensive per capita, resource-limited by staff capacity, and cannot serve most of the demand the clinic surfaces — the intake pipeline produces a documented under-served population the strategy cannot itself resolve without funder investment orders of magnitude larger than the movement currently commands. The strategy is also **hard to fundraise** in movement-philanthropy registers that prefer scalable systemic wins over per-client service, and law-school clinics operate on academic calendars that constrain caseload predictability. And the clinic model risks a **service-organising split**: a clinic that treats representation as end-service, disconnected from the movement's political arms, becomes an isolated professional-service unit; a clinic that treats individual cases as instrumental to movement wins risks the client's own interest in their case getting subordinated to the movement's litigation strategy, which is a real ethical hazard the clinical-legal-education tradition takes seriously.

## Ecology

**Distinct from [strategic litigation against algorithmic state decisions](strat-strategic-litigation-against-algorithmic-state-decisions.md) and [class-action litigation against private AI](strat-class-action-litigation-against-private-ai.md).** Strategic and class litigation bring movement-shaped cases the movement chose to bring for precedent, systemic remedy, or damage aggregation. Clinical representation brings *the individual client's own case* on the client's own terms and interests. The two are different lawyering registers with different professional ethics (client-centred vs. cause-centred), and a movement that carries only the strategic arm without the clinical arm has no answer to the individual client the strategy left unrepresented.

**Feeds [judicial education on AI](strat-judicial-education-on-ai.md) and [public-interest technologist pipeline building](strat-public-interest-technologist-pipeline-building.md).** The clinical bar is where the next generation of AI-fluent attorneys is trained, and the individual-case work is where the working knowledge of what AI harm looks like in the field accumulates — knowledge the judicial-education curriculum draws from, and knowledge the placement pipeline sends into agencies, congressional staff, and judicial clerkships. A movement with strong clinical infrastructure produces a bench pipeline as a byproduct of the service work; a movement without it depends on private-firm associates who happen to develop AI competence on their own trajectories.

**Complementary to [AI-generated image abuse response infrastructure](strat-ai-generated-image-abuse-response-infrastructure.md) and [survivor-led testimony as evidence](strat-survivor-led-testimony-as-evidence.md).** The response infrastructure and survivor testimony are the movement's harm-specific service and evidentiary strategies; the AI-harm clinic is the general legal-service infrastructure they route into for representation. The abuse-response hotline that identifies a survivor needing a civil-litigation or criminal-complaint pathway hands the case to a clinic; the survivor whose testimony carries a legislative campaign is often a client the clinic represented.
