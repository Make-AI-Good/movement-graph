# Masakhane Decolonise AI campaign (2020–ongoing)

[Masakhane](../organizations/org-masakhane-nlp.md) has run an ongoing campaign since 2020 to reorient African-language AI development around community ownership and data sovereignty — rejecting the extractive model in which African language speakers generate data that platforms commodify, and building instead toward AI systems Africans own and govern. The campaign formalizes Masakhane's founding ethical stance, articulated in the [2020 foundational machine translation paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.11529), into specific infrastructure, governance documents, and translation projects.

## Masakhane Ethical Manifesto

The campaign's governance anchor is the **Masakhane Ethical Manifesto**, which codifies the community's data sovereignty principles: data self-determination (Africans decide what data represents their communities and how it is used), consent-based licensing from collection to deployment, and the rejection of existing corpora with contested provenance — the stance that resolved the community's early JW300 corpus controversy, in which data derived from Jehovah's Witnesses scripture was found to carry copyright issues, contract provisions overriding open-access claims, and cross-border consent problems.

## Decolonise Science project (2021–2022)

The campaign's most concrete deliverable is the **Decolonise Science** project, a Lacuna Fund–supported initiative [run in partnership with AfricArxiv and ScienceLink](https://www.csmonitor.com/Science/2021/1007/Finding-the-words-African-translators-aim-to-decolonize-science). Announced in August 2021, the project translated 180 AfricArxiv preprint papers — drawn from English, French, Arabic, and Portuguese — into six African languages: isiZulu, Northern Sotho, Yoruba, Hausa, Luganda, and Amharic. Outputs included approximately 360,000 sentence pairs of multilingual parallel corpus, scientific terminology glossaries for each language, and the Masakhane Web translation platform enabling human-in-the-loop model evaluation. A parallel effort developed STEM terminology for concepts — including "microbes," "evolution," and "dinosaur" — that had no established equivalents in the target languages, with results submitted to official language glossaries.
