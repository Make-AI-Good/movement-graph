# IFAT India gig-worker algorithmic-accountability campaign (2021–ongoing)

The IFAT India gig-worker algorithmic-accountability campaign is the sustained legal and mobilisation effort by the [Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers](../organizations/org-ifat-india.md) to force algorithmic transparency from India's major delivery and ride-hailing platforms and to establish statutory social-security recognition for gig workers through India's Supreme Court. Its defining legal action is a [public interest litigation filed in September 2021](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/awow-db/ifat-petition/) before the Supreme Court of India, seeking recognition of app-based workers as "unorganised workers" under the Unorganised Workers Social Security Act 2008 and demanding disclosure of the algorithms that govern their wages, task allocation, performance scoring, and deactivations on platforms including Ola, Uber, Zomato, and Swiggy. Alongside the PIL, the campaign runs a parallel mobilisation track — worker strikes and bike rallies coordinating across major Indian cities — that applies direct industrial pressure while the judicial track advances.

## The Supreme Court petition (September 2021–ongoing)

IFAT [filed its public interest litigation in September 2021](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/awow-db/ifat-petition/), centering the petition on the algorithmic opacity built into platform operations. The petition identifies fare fixation, promotional costs, surge pricing, incentives, penalties, bonuses, and the allocation of gigs as decision domains where platform algorithms govern worker income and access to work without any worker-facing transparency or procedural recourse. The underlying claim is that these algorithmic systems exercise de facto employer authority over workers' working lives while the platforms maintain that the workers are independent contractors outside the scope of statutory employment and social-security protections.

IFAT's core demands as articulated to [MediaNama in January 2025](https://www.medianama.com/2025/01/223-why-the-gig-workers-union-is-calling-for-algorithmic-data-transparency/) require platforms to make algorithms governing pay, working hours, task allocation, and deactivation fully transparent and accessible to workers, and to share comprehensive payout data and performance-related information. The demands frame algorithmic opacity as the central mechanism through which platforms concentrate power asymmetrically against workers, and the transparency demand as the structural remedy rather than a request for rate increases alone.

The Supreme Court [heard arguments in November 2024](https://www.medianama.com/2024/11/223-supreme-court-hears-plea-social-security-rights-gig-platform-workers/), taking up the plea covering workers on Ola, Uber, Zomato, and Swiggy. The petition remains active and undecided as of late 2026.

## Mobilisation track

Alongside litigation, IFAT combines the Supreme Court arc with direct worker mobilisation. The campaign's largest recorded mobilisation action is the [25 December 2025 flash strike](https://progressive.international/wire/2025-12-29-indias-gig-workers-strike-for-dignity-and-protection/en/), in which approximately 40,000 delivery workers across Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, and Bengaluru refused orders on Swiggy, Zomato, Zepto, Blinkit, Amazon, and Flipkart, with over 2,000 workers joining a bike rally in Hyderabad alone. Demands included fair wages, elimination of unsafe ten-minute delivery targets, ending arbitrary algorithmic penalties, and comprehensive social security — connecting the specific algorithmic-transparency demand to the broader absence of social protection that the PIL seeks to address through statute. Co-founder Shaik Salauddin framed the action as a response to workers pushed to the breaking point by unsafe work models, falling incomes, and the total absence of social protection.

## Corpus position

The IFAT campaign is the corpus's primary South Asian entry on the gig-worker algorithmic-management axis, and the first documented case of a South Asian trade federation using public interest litigation before a national supreme court to demand algorithmic transparency from private platforms. Its structural shape — a federation-led Supreme Court PIL seeking statutory recognition and algorithmic disclosure, combined with direct industrial action — is analogous to the [Worker Info Exchange / ADCU Amsterdam litigation](camp-worker-info-exchange-uber-driver-data-rights-uk.md) in the UK and EU, which used GDPR Article 22 as the statutory hook for comparable algorithmic-transparency demands against Uber and Ola. The two campaigns document the same structural demand (worker visibility into algorithmic management systems) routed through the distinct legal instruments available in their respective jurisdictions.
