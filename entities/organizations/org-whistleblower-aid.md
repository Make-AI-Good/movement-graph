# Whistleblower Aid

Whistleblower Aid is a Washington, D.C.–based nonprofit founded in September 2017 to provide free legal representation, security assistance, and coordinated public advocacy to government and private-sector whistleblowers in the United States. Unlike legal organizations that limit their work to courtroom representation, Whistleblower Aid offers a full-spectrum service model — legal strategy, media and communications planning, congressional briefing coordination, digital and physical security interventions, and psychosocial support — that treats each whistleblower case as a public-interest campaign. [Approximately half of the organization's caseload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) is in the technology sector, covering disclosures from Facebook, Twitter, and Airbnb, positioning Whistleblower Aid as one of the primary legal-defense institutions for tech-industry insiders in the United States seeking to expose harm safely and lawfully.

## Founding and leadership

Whistleblower Aid was [founded in September 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) by John N. Tye and attorney Mark S. Zaid. Tye, who served as section chief for Internet freedom in the State Department's Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor from 2011 to 2014, is himself a government surveillance whistleblower who went public with concerns about NSA surveillance programs — making the founding an act of operational conviction rather than institutional mandate. Zaid brings specialized expertise in national security law and classified-information whistleblower representation, including experience navigating the Intelligence Community Whistleblower Protection Act.

Libby Liu joined as chief executive officer in [April 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid), bringing a background in building global human rights infrastructure. She previously created and led the Open Technology Fund (OTF), a U.S. government–supported program that supports open-source security and privacy tools now used by [more than two billion people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) daily, including Signal, Tor, and WireGuard. Liu's background in digital-security tool distribution, press freedom, and information-operations countermeasures oriented Whistleblower Aid toward the digital security dimensions of high-stakes disclosure — the encrypted intake channels, device hardening, and anonymization practices that AI-era whistleblower cases increasingly require.

## Services model

Whistleblower Aid's service architecture is explicitly broader than legal counsel. Its service provision covers the full disclosure lifecycle: initial secure contact and case intake via encrypted channels; legal strategy and representation in regulatory filings (SEC, FTC, OSHA, and congressional oversight bodies); document-redaction management for congressional submissions; media and communications strategy, including rapid-response planning and press coordination; [congressional briefing logistics and testimony preparation](https://whistlebloweraid.org/about/); and, where appropriate, connection to public advocacy channels. For clients facing financial hardship during and after disclosure, the organization provides limited financial assistance for rent, mortgage, and legal costs, alongside employment networking and psychosocial counseling referrals.

The organization's working model holds that meaningful accountability from a whistleblower disclosure rarely comes from the legal act alone: it requires a coordinated multi-channel campaign in which legal protection makes public testimony possible, media coverage builds public pressure, and congressional engagement translates disclosure into oversight action. Whistleblower Aid functions as the organizing infrastructure for that full campaign.

## Notable cases

The Frances Haugen engagement in 2021 is Whistleblower Aid's most consequential case to date and the clearest demonstration of its full-service model. Haugen, a Facebook product manager who spent two years documenting the company's internal research on algorithmic harm, approached Whistleblower Aid before going public. The organization assembled a team of [three law firms, a public relations firm, and temporary support staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) to coordinate document redaction for the Senate, prepare and manage Haugen's public testimony before the Senate Commerce Committee in October 2021, and organize parallel appearances before parliamentary bodies in the United Kingdom and the European Union. Haugen's disclosures placed Facebook's internal research on algorithmic harm in the permanent public record, reshaped global regulatory discussions about social media accountability, and contributed to the passage of the EU's Digital Services Act.

A 2019 case, from the organization's founding phase, involved the anonymous intelligence community official whose disclosures about President Trump's communications with the Ukrainian government triggered the first presidential impeachment inquiry — establishing Whistleblower Aid's capacity to manage disclosures of the highest political sensitivity.

Joohn Choe, a Facebook contractor, was represented by Whistleblower Aid in [December 2021 and February 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) in complaints alleging Meta had violated U.S. sanctions by permitting pro-Russian rebel propaganda on its platform.

## Tech and AI whistleblower focus

With [approximately half of its caseload in the technology sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid), Whistleblower Aid has built direct experience navigating the distinctive legal terrain of tech-industry disclosures: broad non-disclosure agreements, non-disparagement clauses tied to equity vesting, trade-secret claims, and the informal industry blacklisting that makes legal exposure and employment consequences inseparable. This experience directly maps to the deterrent stack facing AI-industry insiders — the same NDA architecture, equity-vesting leverage, and blacklisting dynamics documented in the 2024 OpenAI departures that prompted the "Right to Warn" open letter from thirteen OpenAI and Google DeepMind employees.

[The AI Whistleblower Initiative (AIWI)](../organizations/org-ai-whistleblower-initiative.md), the Berlin-based nonprofit that supports concerned employees at frontier AI companies, [lists Whistleblower Aid as a vetted legal resource](https://aiwi.org/whistleblower-aid/) for AI insiders navigating safety disclosures — placing Whistleblower Aid inside the AI whistleblower support network alongside [Government Accountability Project](../organizations/org-government-accountability-project.md)'s statutory shield advocacy and [Freedom of the Press Foundation](../organizations/org-freedom-of-the-press-foundation.md)'s SecureDrop document-submission infrastructure. In June 2024, CEO Libby Liu [publicly discussed](https://asianamericapodcast.com/2024/06/ep-481-ceo-libby-liu-on-whistleblower-aid-ndas-radio-free-asia/) Whistleblower Aid's evolving engagement with AI and tech NDA enforcement — specifically the chilling effect of non-disparagement clauses on AI safety disclosures — at the moment when the OpenAI NDA controversy was dominating public discussion of AI insider accountability.
