# Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers

The Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers (IFAT) is India's national federation organizing gig and platform workers — ride-hailing drivers, delivery riders, and courier workers — against the algorithmic management systems that govern their wages, work allocation, and employment security. Founded at a [conference in Mumbai on 19 December 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Federation_of_App-based_Transport_Workers), IFAT is the corpus's primary South Asian entry on the gig-worker algorithmic-management axis: its 2021 Supreme Court petition directly targeted platform algorithmic opacity, it has mobilized workers across [Ola, Uber, Swiggy, Zomato, Zepto, Blinkit, Amazon, and Flipkart](https://progressive.international/wire/2025-12-29-indias-gig-workers-strike-for-dignity-and-protection/en/), and it holds the corpus's only documented [ITF Global](https://www.itfglobal.org/en/affiliates/indian-federation-app-based-transport-workers) affiliation among Indian platform-worker organizations.

## Founding and leadership

IFAT was established at its founding conference in Mumbai on 19 December 2019. Prashant Bhagesh Sawardekar founded the federation and serves as national president; [Shaik Salauddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaik_Salauddin) co-founded IFAT and serves as national general secretary, with Udaykumar Ambonkar serving in the same capacity since 2023.

Salauddin's organizing background shaped IFAT's character from the start. Having worked on app-based ride-hailing platforms in Hyderabad since 2012, he [founded the Telangana State Taxi and Drivers Joint Action Committee in 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaik_Salauddin) — unifying twenty driver associations across the state — and later launched the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union in 2022. His sustained organizing across driver associations, court petitions, and state government engagement gave IFAT an organizer-led character, and his direct advocacy for the Rajasthan and Karnataka Platform-based Gig Workers Bills anchors IFAT's legislative strategy alongside its judicial one.

IFAT claims a membership of [approximately 156,000 workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Federation_of_App-based_Transport_Workers) and is affiliated with the [International Transport Workers Federation](https://www.itfglobal.org/en/affiliates/indian-federation-app-based-transport-workers) in the Road Transport sector.

## Algorithmic transparency demands and Supreme Court petition

IFAT's central organizational demand is algorithmic transparency: the federation calls for platforms to ensure that [algorithms governing pay, working hours, task allocation, and deactivation are fully transparent and accessible to workers](https://www.medianama.com/2025/01/223-why-the-gig-workers-union-is-calling-for-algorithmic-data-transparency/). The immediate problem is opacity: workers cannot see how rides or orders are allocated to them, how pay rates are calculated, how rating systems affect future access to work, or how performance metrics trigger penalties or deactivations.

In September 2021, IFAT [filed public interest litigation before India's Supreme Court](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/awow-db/ifat-petition/) seeking recognition of gig workers as unorganised workers under the Unorganised Workers Social Security Act 2008. The petition documented the algorithmic opacity built into platform operations — targeting specifically the lack of transparency around fare fixation, promotional costs, surge pricing, incentives, penalties, bonuses, and allocation of gigs — and framed this opacity as a governance problem requiring judicial remedy. The Supreme Court [heard arguments in November 2024](https://www.medianama.com/2024/11/223-supreme-court-hears-plea-social-security-rights-gig-platform-workers/) as the petition remained active, covering workers on Ola, Uber, Zomato, and Swiggy.

## Strike actions and worker mobilization

IFAT combines legal advocacy with direct worker mobilization. In June 2020, the federation organized [nationwide protests drawing over 38,000 workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Federation_of_App-based_Transport_Workers) during the COVID-19 pandemic, demanding personal protective equipment and government insurance for gig workers. Subsequent actions in September 2020 (Swiggy delivery workers against payment structure changes) and February 2021 (multi-state strikes across twelve or more cities demanding fare increases to offset fuel costs) demonstrated IFAT capacity to mobilize across platforms and geographies.

The federation's largest recorded action was the [25 December 2025 flash strike](https://progressive.international/wire/2025-12-29-indias-gig-workers-strike-for-dignity-and-protection/en/), in which approximately 40,000 delivery workers across Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, and Bengaluru refused orders on Swiggy, Zomato, Zepto, Blinkit, Amazon, and Flipkart. Over 2,000 workers joined a bike rally in Hyderabad alone. Demands included fair wages, elimination of unsafe ten-minute delivery targets, ending arbitrary algorithmic penalties, improved safety equipment, and comprehensive social security. Shaik Salauddin framed the action as a response to workers being pushed to the breaking point by unsafe work models, falling incomes, and the total absence of social protection — connecting the specific algorithmic-management complaint (arbitrary penalties, opaque deactivations) to the broader absence of social safety nets, which is IFAT's dual legislative-advocacy target.

## Movement position and India context

IFAT operates in a platform-worker organizing environment where India's gig workforce numbers in the tens of millions and where the legal classification of app-based workers as independent contractors systematically excludes them from the labor protections held by the organized workforce. The federation's dual track — Supreme Court litigation seeking statutory recognition alongside direct mobilization — mirrors approaches used by European platform-worker organizing bodies such as [Worker Info Exchange](../organizations/org-worker-info-exchange.md) in the UK and EU, but is adapted to the Indian context where no GDPR-equivalent data-rights law provides a statutory hook, and where the judicial route runs through the Supreme Court public interest litigation jurisdiction. IFAT's specific articulation of the algorithmic-transparency demand — connecting the opacity of fare algorithms, surge pricing, and allocation systems to the structural power asymmetry between workers and platforms — makes it the corpus's primary South Asian organizational anchor on the algorithmic-management axis, closing the India gig-worker labor geo-gap in the worker-organizing coverage area.
