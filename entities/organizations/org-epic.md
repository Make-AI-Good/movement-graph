# Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC)

The Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC) is a Washington, D.C.–based public interest research and advocacy center [founded in 1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Privacy_Information_Center) to defend privacy, freedom of expression, and democratic values against commercial and government surveillance. It is the United States' most established nonprofit organization focused on the intersection of privacy rights and algorithmic accountability, operating across litigation, regulatory advocacy, model legislation, coalition building, and civil society capacity-funding. EPIC works at the technical policy layer of the US AI accountability ecosystem, translating consumer-rights advocacy into FTC enforcement actions, legislative campaigns, and state AG mobilization that shape how AI systems are governed and deployed.

## Origins and structure

EPIC was established in 1994 in Washington, D.C., originally with support from the [Fund for Constitutional Government](https://epic.org/) and Computer Professionals for Social Responsibility. Its founding decade established the organization as the principal civil society interlocutor with the Federal Trade Commission on privacy issues, developing the FTC's authority to address emerging privacy enforcement through formal complaint proceedings. Alan Butler has served as Executive Director and President since 2021, with Caitriona Fitzgerald as Deputy Director and Policy Director and John Davisson as Deputy Director and Director of Enforcement. EPIC's advisory board has included privacy scholars Shoshana Zuboff and Danielle Citron.

EPIC organizes its substantive work across nine program areas: Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Consumer Privacy, Cybersecurity, Data Protection, Democracy and Free Speech, Open Government, Platform Accountability and Governance, Privacy Laws, and Surveillance Oversight. Its Global Privacy Fund (formerly Public Voice Fund) provides grants to civil society organizations internationally to support public participation in internet governance and AI accountability processes, having funded organizations in more than 30 countries.

## FTC complaint practice and corporate accountability

EPIC's signature operational method is the formal FTC complaint — a mechanism that places a regulatory dossier before an enforcement agency and creates public accountability pressure even when a formal investigation does not result. The approach has produced concrete behavioral change: after EPIC filed a complaint against hiring technology company HireVue alleging its AI-based facial scanning of job applicants was unfair and discriminatory, [HireVue announced it would halt facial analysis](https://epic.org/hirevue-facing-ftc-complaint-from-epic-halts-use-of-facial-recognition/) entirely. EPIC has filed formal complaints against Facebook, Snapchat, WhatsApp, Google, and Microsoft for privacy violations, and [written to FTC Chair Ferguson in February 2026](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/letter-from-EPIC-to-FTC-re-Meta-FRT-glasses.pdf) demanding investigation of Meta's plan to add facial recognition capabilities to its Ray-Ban glasses. In the surveillance domain, EPIC's litigation produced the removal of airport body scanners (*EPIC v. DHS*) and compelled NSA cybersecurity authority disclosure (*EPIC v. NSA*). EPIC also maintains an amicus program that has filed approximately 100 friend-of-the-court briefs across federal and state courts.

## Algorithmic accountability legislation

EPIC has been a lead civil society advocate for algorithmic transparency legislation since the Algorithmic Accountability Act was first introduced in 2019, providing the legislative research and advocacy that shaped the bill's impact-assessment framework. When [Rep. Yvette Clarke and Sen. Ron Wyden re-introduced the bill in 2025](https://epic.org/epic-endorses-algorithmic-accountability-act-of-2025/), EPIC formally endorsed it and documented its role in developing the bill's requirement that companies assess whether automated decision systems are "inaccurate, unfair, biased, or discriminatory" in consequential domains including employment, housing, education, and healthcare.

In parallel, EPIC has advanced state-level legislation through model bill development. In September 2024, EPIC co-released the [State Data Privacy Act](https://epic.org/the-state-data-privacy-act-a-proposed-model-state-privacy-bill/) with Consumer Reports — a comprehensive model privacy bill for state legislatures — building a coalition of endorsers including Accountable Tech, the Center for Democracy and Technology, Common Sense Media, the Consumer Federation of America, Issue One, US PIRG, and Public Knowledge. The bill was updated in April 2025. EPIC has also published a [State AG Privacy Enforcement report (October 2025)](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/EPIC-State-AG-Enforcement.pdf) cataloguing more than 220 enforcement cases and settlements from state attorneys general between January 2020 and December 2024, supporting AG capacity and mobilization to enforce privacy rights in the absence of federal legislation.

## Worker surveillance and AI union suppression

A significant recent area of EPIC's work addresses the use of AI and algorithmic management tools to suppress worker organizing — applying EPIC's privacy-rights framework to workplace power dynamics. In February 2026, EPIC and the [National Institute for Workers' Rights](https://epic.org/press-release-epic-and-the-national-institute-for-workers-rights-release-white-paper-on-algorithmic-unionbusting/) released "Who's Got the Power? Restoring Worker Power in the Age of Algorithmic Unionbusting," documenting how productivity monitoring software, biometric surveillance, AI-powered management tools, and predictive analytics have enabled employers to identify, monitor, and interfere with workers seeking to organize. The paper argues that neither existing labor law nor the patchwork of US privacy laws adequately addresses these practices. EPIC also [endorsed federal legislation](https://epic.org/epic-endorses-federal-bills-barring-worker-surveillance-automated-workplace-decisions/) — the Worker Privacy Act and Stop Spying Bosses Act — barring employer surveillance and automated workplace decisions in the context of labor rights.

## Public Voice Coalition and international civil society

In 1996, EPIC established the [Public Voice Coalition](https://epic.org/) with initial support from the Ford Foundation and Open Society Institute, creating an international civil society network to amplify non-commercial voices in internet governance decisions. The Public Voice has engaged civil society organizations at OECD, UN, and ITU processes on AI governance, privacy, and digital rights, helping channel grassroots and civil society perspectives into global standard-setting. EPIC's related Public Voice Fund has provided small grants to civil society organizations to participate in internet governance and AI accountability meetings internationally, building organizational capacity in regions where advocacy infrastructure for digital rights is thin.
