# The Intercept

The Intercept is a New York-based nonprofit investigative newsroom [launched in February 2014](https://theintercept.com/about/) by Glenn Greenwald, Laura Poitras, and Jeremy Scahill. It became an [independent nonprofit in January 2023](https://theintercept.com/2023/01/09/intercept-restructuring-nonprofit/), separating from Pierre Omidyar's First Look Media after Omidyar withdrew major backing in late 2022. Its AI-harm journalism spans three overlapping domains — algorithmic warfare, predictive-policing infrastructure, and military AI contracting — sustained over more than a decade from the 2015 Drone Papers through the 2026 Pentagon–OpenAI investigation, making it one of the most durable producers of original AI-harm investigation among U.S. nonprofit newsrooms. In scope on the 2026-09-01 accountability-journalism statute, on-ramp-b.

## Founding and structure

Omidyar announced First Look Media in 2013 with a $250 million commitment to investigative journalism. The Intercept launched on February 10, 2014, initially as a vehicle for publishing and contextualizing documents leaked by NSA whistleblower Edward Snowden. Co-founders Greenwald (constitutional lawyer and journalist), Poitras (documentary filmmaker, *Citizenfour*), and Scahill (author of *Dirty Wars*) assembled a national security and civil liberties team that expanded into accountability reporting on policing, immigration, and technology.

The Intercept restructured as an independent nonprofit in January 2023, with First Look providing a multiyear transition gift. The outlet had built one of the largest membership programs among U.S. nonprofit newsrooms during its First Look era; the independent phase relies on reader membership and philanthropic grants. As of 2026, Ben Muessig serves as editor-in-chief and Annie Chabel as CEO.

## The Drone Papers and algorithmic warfare

The Intercept's ["Drone Papers"](https://theintercept.com/drone-papers/) (October 2015) is among the earliest major works of original investigative journalism documenting AI-driven harm in warfare. A JSOC whistleblower provided classified documents detailing U.S. drone targeting methodology in Afghanistan, Yemen, and Somalia. [The lead article](https://theintercept.com/drone-papers/the-assassination-complex/) documented that during five months of Operation Haymaker in northeastern Afghanistan, approximately 90 percent of people killed by U.S. drone strikes were not the intended targets — a direct consequence of the military's reliance on cellphone SIM metadata to infer target identity and location rather than human-intelligence confirmation. Individuals were added to kill lists not because they had been individually identified but because a phone associated with a target's communication network was geolocated to an area; the inference was algorithmic and automated. The papers were reported by Jeremy Scahill, Ryan Devereaux, and colleagues, generated Congressional attention and international legal commentary on the accountability deficits of algorithmic targeting, and were published in book form as *The Assassination Complex* (2016).

## Palantir and predictive policing

In February 2017, The Intercept revealed [how Palantir had integrated into the NSA's mass-surveillance pipeline](https://theintercept.com/2017/02/22/how-peter-thiels-palantir-helped-the-nsa-spy-on-the-whole-world/) through documents leaked from GCHQ: Palantir had built XKEYSCORE Helper, software enabling NSA analysts to import data from XKEYSCORE — the NSA's widest-reaching surveillance program — directly into the Palantir analysis environment, embedding Palantir in the collection-to-analysis pipeline for signals intelligence.

In May 2018, The Intercept [documented the LAPD's use of Palantir for predictive policing](https://theintercept.com/2018/05/11/predictive-policing-surveillance-los-angeles/): Palantir's platform generated "chronic offender" scores from criminal history, license plate reader data, and social media, and officers were dispatched to visit scored individuals at home to notify them of their surveillance status — an enforcement mechanism built on algorithmic designation rather than observed behavior. A January 2021 follow-up, ["How LAPD and Palantir Use Data to Justify Racist Policing,"](https://theintercept.com/2021/01/30/lapd-palantir-data-driven-policing/) documented that chronic-offender designations fell overwhelmingly on Black and Latino individuals in historically over-policed communities and that the department cited algorithmic outputs to justify enforcement patterns rather than evaluate them — a feedback loop that produced and then used algorithmic confirmation for racially disparate policing.

In September 2026, The Intercept reported that [ICE granted new hires access to restricted personal data in Palantir's ELITE deportation-targeting app before those employees had completed background checks](https://theintercept.com/2026/09/03/ice-palantir-privacy-personal-info-access/). ELITE assigns AI-generated confidence scores to potential deportation targets; the DHS privacy impact assessment on file links to a 2019 document that "completely fails to describe the app's data sources, its AI analysis, or the inferences it produces."

## Pentagon AI and military contracting

Through FOIA litigation — Freedom of Information Act lawsuits rather than voluntary disclosure — The Intercept has built an extended investigative record on AI companies' relationships with the U.S. military. Documents obtained through this litigation revealed in September 2026 that [the Department of Defense had asked OpenAI to provide a version of its AI technology with "minimal refusal rates" for military commands](https://theintercept.com/2026/09/08/pentagon-openai-military-contract/). Additional records showed that [OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI are contractually embedded with the military](https://theintercept.com/2026/09/08/military-ai-weapons-contracts-openai-anthropic-google/), with OpenAI agreeing to embed company engineers within military units. In March 2026, The Intercept reported that [OpenAI and Anthropic's public statements on autonomous weapons and surveillance diverged from their active military contract terms](https://theintercept.com/2026/03/08/openai-anthropic-military-contract-ethics-surveillance/): Anthropic had refused Pentagon terms requiring unrestricted deployment on classified networks and was subsequently designated a "supply chain risk" by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

## Flock AI surveillance

In 2026, The Intercept published an extended series on Flock Safety, the AI-powered automated license plate reader company contracted with thousands of U.S. law enforcement agencies. Public records requests revealed that [a single town in Illinois had conducted more than 1,200 Flock searches investigating loitering cases](https://theintercept.com/2026/09/09/flock-cameras-police-loitering-curfew-surveillance/) — a non-criminal activity — showing that the AI surveillance platform's use had expanded far beyond the violent-crime use cases Flock had used to market the technology. Subsequent investigations documented Flock's financial contributions to local politicians' associations as community opposition grew, and the company's use of an AI-assisted nonprofit to manufacture the appearance of grassroots support in target markets.

## Role in the movement

The Intercept's contribution to the AI-harm movement runs through original investigation: the Drone Papers established that algorithmic targeting in warfare produces systematic civilian harm at scale; the Palantir series produced the first detailed public documentation of predictive policing infrastructure and its racial feedback loop; and the Pentagon AI series constitutes the most extensive public record of military AI contracting produced by a U.S. nonprofit newsroom. Its FOIA litigation capability — obtaining through lawsuits what AI companies would not voluntarily release — functions as a standing accountability infrastructure for AI deployed by military and immigration enforcement. These three domains (warfare, policing, and immigration enforcement) constitute a sustained accountability beat maintained at greater scope and duration than any other U.S. nonprofit newsroom, and The Intercept's investigations have provided foundational empirical material for peer organizations including [ProPublica](../organizations/org-propublica.md), [The Markup](../organizations/org-the-markup.md), and [Bellingcat](../organizations/org-bellingcat.md).
